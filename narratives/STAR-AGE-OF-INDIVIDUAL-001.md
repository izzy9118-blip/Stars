# THE AGE OF THE INDIVIDUAL

*Power, Freedom, and the Long History of the Self*

In the age of the individual, what you felt and what you wanted and what you dreamed of were going to become the driving force across the world. And to understand the present, you have to go back and see what happened when those hopes and dreams and uncertainties inside people’s minds met the much older forces of power.

Those forces began in the sacred order that first made the world intelligible. The sacred was not originally one belief among others inside an individual mind; it was the structure through which reality itself appeared. Sacred places established a center around which the world could be inhabited, sacred times returned human beings to the beginnings of things, myths revealed how the world and its forms of life had come into existence, and rituals repeated the acts of gods and ancestors so that the order of the cosmos could be renewed. Birth, sexuality, hunting, agriculture, kingship, suffering, and death belonged to a reality that preceded the individual and gave every action its meaning. Power first appeared not merely as the command of one human being over another but as the authority of a sacred order that determined what was real, where human beings belonged, and how their lives were connected to the cosmos.

That sacred order entered the household through the worship of the dead and the family fire. The dead remained near the living, their tombs bound a family to the soil, and the hearth became the dwelling place of ancestors whose worship had to be continued from one generation to the next. The father was at once priest, judge, and ruler because he guarded the rites upon which the continuity of the family depended. Marriage brought the wife into the husband’s cult, inheritance passed responsibility for the sacred fire to the proper heir, adoption preserved the worship of a family that might otherwise disappear, and property became inseparable from the graves, gods, and rituals rooted in the land. Law did not first arise as an agreement among independent individuals; it emerged from religious distinctions governing kinship, authority, succession, property, and the obligations owed to the dead.

Families joined into larger religious associations, and their gods, sacrifices, calendars, and ancestral laws were gathered into the polis. The ancient city was not merely a collection of private persons protected by a government; it was a sacred community that united religion, law, territory, ancestry, and political authority. Citizenship meant participation in the common worship, and exclusion from the worship meant exclusion from the city. The citizen belonged to the polis in body and soul because the city claimed his military service, public conduct, family relations, education, and reverence for its gods. Political power was therefore not external to the citizen’s identity: the city formed the world within which he understood himself.

The growth of the polis also transformed authority within the older household order. Kings, aristocratic families, assemblies, magistrates, written laws, and popular classes struggled over who would rule and on what claim. Religious authority did not disappear, but ancestral privilege was increasingly contested by common citizenship and public law. The political question became inseparable from the question of the regime: which part of the city ruled, which human qualities it honored, how offices and honors were distributed, and what way of life the city held to be good.

The plurality of cities, gods, customs, and ancestral laws then gave rise to a question that the sacred city could not contain. The discovery of nature opened a distinction between what existed by nature and what existed only by convention. If different cities worshipped different gods and called different ways of life just, ancestral authority could no longer prove that any particular ancestral law was true. Philosophy began when human beings sought an account of the whole that did not depend upon the inherited opinions of any one city.

As tradition tells it, Socrates brought philosophy down from the heavens and into the city by turning the question of nature toward human things. He asked what justice, courage, moderation, law, virtue, and the good actually were. The question was no longer merely what Athens commanded, but whether the commands of Athens were just. Socrates began from the authoritative opinions of the city because those opinions contained partial and contradictory intimations of the questions they attempted to answer. Through conversation he exposed their contradictions, not merely to destroy them, but to replace confident ignorance with awareness of the problem.

The trial of Socrates revealed the permanent tension between philosophy and the city. The city required authoritative opinions capable of forming character, sustaining obedience, distinguishing friend from enemy, and binding citizens together. Philosophy examined those opinions in light of a truth that might transcend the city. The philosopher needed the peace, language, laws, and education of political society, yet the freedom of inquiry could call the city’s gods, heroes, and claims to justice into question.

Plato presented this tension through dialogues rather than a doctrine announced in his own name. The conversations joined argument to character, circumstance, irony, and action, revealing that political teaching could not be separated from the souls of the people receiving it. The Republic traced the correspondence between the city and the soul: every regime elevated a particular human type, honored particular desires, and educated its citizens toward a particular conception of happiness. Timocracy honored spirited ambition, oligarchy wealth, democracy freedom and equality, and tyranny the unlimited rule of desire. Political institutions and individual character were different expressions of the same order.

Justice therefore meant more than obedience to existing law. In the soul it required the proper ordering of reason, spirit, and desire; in the city it required that functions be performed according to knowledge and aptitude rather than wealth, birth, or appetite. The philosopher appeared as the person capable of judging political life because philosophy turned away from the changing opinions inside the cave toward knowledge of what was good by nature. Yet the philosopher’s knowledge did not easily become political rule. The coincidence of philosophy and political power required by the best city was exceedingly improbable, and the best regime existed primarily in speech. Its apparent perfection disclosed both the standard by which cities could be judged and the limits of what political action could accomplish.

The allegory of the cave revealed that political power worked through the formation of perception as well as through coercion. Human beings ordinarily encountered images, stories, laws, honors, and inherited opinions before they could examine the truth of what those things represented. Education was therefore a turning of the soul, but the philosopher who returned to the cave encountered resistance because the city’s shared images were also the foundation of its order. The problem of power already included the power to shape the horizon within which citizens distinguished the noble from the shameful, the just from the unjust, and the real from the unreal.

Aristotle brought the inquiry back from the best city in speech to the variety of actual regimes. The polis existed by nature because it completed the associations beginning in the household and village and because human beings possessed speech through which they could deliberate about the advantageous, the just, and the good. The city was prior to the isolated individual in the sense that only within a political community could distinctively human capacities be fully developed. A human being separated from political life was not the naturally independent individual imagined by modern philosophy, but either less than human or more than human.

A regime was the arrangement of the city’s offices, especially its ruling authority, and the identity of the ruling part determined the character and end of the political community. Kingship, aristocracy, and polity claimed to rule for the common good, while tyranny, oligarchy, and democracy in their defective forms ruled for the advantage of one ruler, the wealthy, or the poor. The dispute among regimes was a dispute among competing claims to justice: freedom, wealth, birth, virtue, and numerical equality each contained part of the truth while mistaking that part for the whole.

Political judgment therefore required prudence rather than the mechanical application of a universal formula. The best regime in itself was not always the best regime attainable under particular circumstances, and the legislator had to understand the character, size, resources, divisions, and habits of an actual people. Law made reason durable through institutions and habituation, but no law could anticipate every circumstance. The prudent statesman united knowledge of general ends with judgment about particular situations.

Ethics and politics formed a continuous inquiry because regimes educated desire. Moral virtue was acquired through habituation, and political institutions taught citizens what to love, fear, honor, and despise. Yet moral and political life did not exhaust human perfection. Contemplation remained the highest activity because it approached what was most self-sufficient and enduring, while political life remained dependent upon circumstance, action, conflict, and other people. Aristotle therefore preserved the natural dignity of the polis while maintaining the distinction between the good citizen, whose virtue depended partly upon the regime, and the good human being, whose excellence could not be reduced to any existing political order.

Cicero carried Greek political philosophy into the Roman world by joining natural right to republican government. A republic was the common concern of a people united not merely by force or proximity but by agreement concerning justice and common advantage. True law was right reason in agreement with nature, not one command in Rome and another in Athens, nor one rule today and another tomorrow. No ruler, assembly, or people could make injustice just merely by commanding it.

The mixed regime attempted to combine the authority of one, the excellence of the few, and the consent of the many so that each power checked the degeneration of the others. Civic freedom depended upon law, offices, customs, property, and a ruling class educated toward public responsibility. Cicero thus transmitted the classical claim that political communities could be judged by a justice higher than their positive laws, but he also helped make natural law portable beyond the polis, available to an empire and eventually to religious and modern doctrines that would give it radically different foundations.

Biblical revelation transformed this structure without abolishing the question of authority. The world was no longer simply an eternal cosmos but a creation dependent upon a transcendent God. Divine law addressed human beings from beyond the city, sacred history moved from creation through commandment, covenant, judgment, repentance, and redemption, and rulers and peoples alike stood under divine judgment. The city could no longer present itself as the highest human whole because its laws were answerable to an authority they had not created.

Revealed law claimed authority not merely over public conduct but over the whole of human life. Unlike the ancestral nomos of a particular polis, its truth did not depend upon the customs of one city or the decision of its rulers. Government was placed in the service of a truth that transcended the political community rather than religion being placed entirely in the service of government. The prophetic challenge to kings made political authority answerable to righteousness, while commandment joined worship, morality, family life, property, education, and political order within a comprehensive law.

Maimonides understood the Torah as a law directed toward two human perfections. Its first purpose was the welfare of the body: the prevention of violence, regulation of conduct, formation of moral habits, and establishment of the peace and justice without which human beings could not live together. Its higher purpose was the welfare of the soul: the formation of true opinions and ultimately knowledge and love of God. Political order was indispensable because it created the conditions under which intellectual perfection became possible, but political life was not itself the highest end. The law ordered the body and the community so that the soul could be turned toward the truth.

Prophecy joined knowledge to political foundation. The prophet possessed intellectual perfection and the capacity to communicate truths through images, stories, commandments, rites, promises, and prohibitions capable of guiding the multitude. Prophecy therefore possessed an essentially political dimension and culminated in legislation. Mosaic prophecy stood uniquely at the origin of the law, and Moses was not merely a knower of divine things but the founder of a community ordered toward human perfection. Human law sought security and civil peace; divine law placed that peace in the service of knowledge of God.

Because the law addressed human beings of unequal capacities, it could not teach everyone in the same manner. It contained teachings about God and the order of being, but it also contained beliefs necessary for forming character, sustaining obedience, and preserving the political conditions of human perfection. The deepest questions therefore appeared through parables, images, and figurative language, while the literal teaching supplied the community with an intelligible moral and political world.

The Guide of the Perplexed was addressed to the person whose inherited understanding of the law had been unsettled by philosophy. When demonstration showed that a literal interpretation was impossible, Scripture had to be interpreted figuratively; yet the secrets of the law could not simply be disclosed to everyone without endangering the beliefs upon which the authority of the law and the order of the community depended. Maimonides therefore wrote through hints, omissions, deliberate contradictions, and different levels of teaching, guiding the philosophically perplexed reader while protecting the community from a disclosure it might be unable to absorb.

At the center of this perplexity stood the question of creation. If the world existed eternally and followed necessarily from unchanging causes, divine command, miracle, and revelation risked being absorbed into a natural order accessible to philosophy. If the world had been created through divine will, then the whole could not be made completely intelligible by unaided human reason. Maimonides argued that philosophy had not demonstrated the eternity of the world and therefore could not refute creation, preserving the rational possibility of revelation without transforming revelation into philosophic knowledge.

Athens had asked what way of life and form of rule accorded with nature; Jerusalem asked whether human beings lived under a law given by God. Maimonides joined these questions without abolishing their difference. Platonic political philosophy made it possible to understand prophetic law as an order directed toward human perfection, while the divine law remained the tribunal before which philosophy had to justify its freedom. The encounter produced neither a final synthesis nor a simple victory of one side, but a permanent tension between reason and revelation, free inquiry and faithful obedience.

Modernity began by rebelling against the apparent failure of both classical philosophy and biblical religion to secure human life against violence, fortune, persecution, and misery. Machiavelli rejected the imagined republics and principalities that judged political action by standards rulers could not reliably follow under the pressure of necessity. He turned instead toward the effectual truth of things: how states were actually founded, acquired, preserved, corrupted, and destroyed.

This was not merely the neutral discovery that rulers sometimes acted immorally. It was a deliberate reorientation of political philosophy. If good rulers who refused to learn how not to be good were destroyed by those willing to use force and deception, then political knowledge had to teach founders how to enter evil when necessity required it. Virtù became the capacity to command circumstance, organize force, manipulate appearances, use cruelty decisively, and establish new modes and orders capable of surviving fortune.

Machiavelli lowered the ends of politics in order to increase the probability of their realization. Rather than beginning with the highest human perfection, he began with the acquisition and preservation of political order. The founder replaced the contemplative philosopher as the exemplary political figure, and the conquest of fortune became the political anticipation of the conquest of nature. Power increasingly meant the human capacity to create an order rather than submission to an order given by nature or God.

Machiavelli’s attempt to conquer political fortune became, in Bacon, a project to conquer fortune in nature. Bacon rejected a philosophy that ended in contemplation because it left disease, scarcity, suffering, and chance largely untouched. Knowledge had to prove itself through its works. Experiment would intervene in natural processes, isolate their causes, and discover how effects could be reproduced. Organized across generations and supported by public institutions, science would accumulate discoveries that no solitary philosopher could achieve, turning knowledge into a collective instrument for relieving the human condition. Nature was no longer primarily the permanent order within which human beings discovered their proper ends; it became a field of hidden operations that could be exposed, recombined, and redirected toward ends chosen by human beings.

Descartes gave this project its foundation in the autonomous knowing subject. Methodical doubt suspended the authority of tradition, inherited philosophy, ordinary experience, and even the apparent existence of the external world until certainty was found in the act of thinking itself. Knowledge now began not from the visible order of the cosmos but from the self-certainty of the thinking “I.” The world outside that subject was reconstructed as extension, figure, motion, and mathematically intelligible law. Bodies no longer had to be understood through purposes toward which they naturally moved; they could be explained as mechanisms whose parts produced effects through calculable relations. God still guaranteed the truth of clear and distinct knowledge, but the operative center of inquiry had shifted to a method by which the human mind could reconstruct the world from principles it could certify for itself.

Bacon and Descartes therefore reversed the older relation between human art and nature. Art had once imitated or completed purposes already present in nature; modern science would discover the elementary processes from which natural things were composed and use them to produce effects nature had not produced on its own. The success of knowledge would be measured increasingly by prediction, reproduction, and control. Nature ceased to provide the authoritative standard for human purposes and became the material upon which human purposes acted. Once the natural world could be reconstructed from motion, mechanism, and law, the same possibility could be extended to human beings and political society: passions could be analyzed as forces, institutions designed as machines, and order deliberately constructed from elements that possessed no natural orientation toward a common good.

Grotius helped detach natural law from the comprehensive authority of revelation and the classical hierarchy of human ends. Natural law could be understood through enduring features of human beings, including self-preservation, sociability, the keeping of promises, respect for possession, and responsibility for injuries. Its rules would retain their rational force even under the hypothetical concession that God did not exist or did not concern Himself with human affairs.

The law of nature could therefore regulate relations among individuals and among sovereign states without requiring agreement about revelation or the highest good. Property, contract, promise, punishment, war, and peace were increasingly organized through rights and obligations attributable to separate persons and political bodies. Natural right began to move away from an objective order directing human beings toward perfection and toward a structure of individual claims that political authority was required to recognize.

Hobbes completed the decisive reconstruction. Human beings were imagined before the city as naturally separate, equal in vulnerability, driven by desire and aversion, and exposed to violent death. There was no natural highest good capable of uniting them, because desire moved continually from one object to another and different individuals called different things good. The strongest common passion was not attraction toward perfection but fear of death and the desire for commodious preservation.

Self-preservation became the primary natural right, and right was distinguished from law. Natural right was the liberty to use one’s power for preservation, while natural law was the rational rule directing individuals toward peace when peace was possible. Because promises could not secure themselves in the absence of a common power, individuals authorized a sovereign capable of defining law, judging disputes, punishing disobedience, and representing their united will.

Political obligation no longer arose from sacred inheritance, natural hierarchy, or the purpose of the polis. It arose from the authorization of individuals seeking protection. The individual and his right came before society, while the state became an artificial person constructed to control the conflict generated by naturally independent wills. Sovereignty had to be undivided because rival authorities claiming obedience in the name of civil law and divine law would return the community to war.

Religion was consequently brought under sovereign judgment in its public effects. The sovereign determined which teachings could be publicly authorized because competing claims to speak for God could create competing claims to rule. Inward belief could not be directly commanded, but public doctrine, worship, and ecclesiastical authority had to be subordinated to civil peace. The modern state began to displace revealed law as the comprehensive judge of the public world.

Spinoza carried this modern break into the foundations of revelation itself. Where Maimonides had understood divine law as a political and spiritual order leading human beings from civil peace toward knowledge of God, Spinoza separated philosophy from theology by assigning them different purposes. Philosophy sought truth through reason, while Scripture sought piety and obedience through imagination.

The prophets were not speculative thinkers possessing knowledge beyond the reach of philosophy. They were human beings with unusually powerful imaginations who translated moral commands into images, stories, promises, warnings, and laws suited to the temperaments and historical circumstances of particular peoples. Revelation ceased to be a body of supernatural knowledge and became a practical education teaching the multitude to perform works of justice and charity.

This transformation required the destruction of the traditional meaning of miracle. God and nature could no longer be understood as two powers, with God occasionally interrupting the natural order to reveal His will, because everything followed eternally and necessarily from the divine nature itself. There was no breach in the order of causes and no event that could stand outside nature as evidence of revelation. What people called miracles were natural events whose causes they did not understand, interpreted through fear, hope, and imagination as signs of divine favor or anger.

Spinoza thereby closed the opening Maimonides had preserved between creation and eternity. Maimonides had argued that philosophy could not demonstrate the eternity of the world and therefore had to leave open the possibility of creation, prophecy, miracle, and divine command. Spinoza identified divine action with natural necessity. God ceased to be a transcendent legislator who freely created and governed the world and became the immanent cause from which everything necessarily followed. Good and evil, reward and punishment, providence and purpose no longer belonged to nature itself; they expressed human judgments about whether things increased or diminished human power.

Scripture could consequently be examined as a human book produced within history. Its authors had written for particular audiences under particular political conditions, in languages filled with metaphor and accommodation. Its contradictions, repetitions, vocabularies, and references to later events revealed that the Pentateuch was not simply a text dictated by God and written in its entirety by Moses, but a compilation assembled and transmitted by human beings. Its authority no longer depended upon the scientific or metaphysical truth of its statements. It depended upon its power to produce obedience, justice, charity, and peace.

The same transformation occurred in politics. Spinoza defined natural right through natural power: every being possessed as much right as it possessed the power to exist and act. Before civil society there was no justice or injustice, guilt or innocence, because no moral law stood above the powers operating in nature. Political society did not abolish this natural right; it concentrated and organized it. The sovereign possessed a right extending as far as its effective power to command, while its stability depended upon organizing the passions and interests of the multitude into a power greater than that of any rival authority.

Revealed religion could no longer remain an independent source of law above the state. The sovereign acquired authority over ceremonies, public worship, and the political effects of religious teaching. Yet the state could command actions more easily than beliefs. Attempts to govern inward judgment or suppress thought produced resentment, hypocrisy, and instability, weakening the sovereign that imposed them. Freedom of thought was therefore not merely a private concession but a source of political strength.

Religion nevertheless remained necessary because most human beings were governed more by passion and imagination than by reason. Philosophy could lead the few toward intellectual freedom, but religion translated the conduct demanded by reason into commandments accessible to the multitude. Spinoza distinguished religion from superstition by their effects: superstition arose from fear and produced hatred, persecution, and division, while religion directed imagination toward justice, charity, obedience, and peace. Faith was judged less by the speculative truth of its doctrines than by the conduct it produced.

Particular revelations were thus displaced by a minimal universal faith under which Jews and Christians could live as equal citizens while disagreeing about theology. Religion ceased to constitute the comprehensive public law of the community, philosophy was released from theological supervision, and the liberal state became neutral among rival revelations while protecting the individual’s freedom to think and speak. Yet Spinoza had not conclusively refuted revelation. His critique assumed the authority of autonomous reason and the necessary intelligibility of nature—the very assumptions revealed religion denied. He did not resolve the conflict between reason and revelation; he reorganized political society so that the conflict would no longer determine citizenship.

Locke moderated the radical implications of Hobbes and Spinoza while preserving the priority of the individual. Human beings possessed rights to life, liberty, and property before entering political society, labor made the external world one’s own, and government existed to protect rights it had not created. Consent limited legitimate authority, toleration withdrew inward belief from most forms of political command, and revolution became justified when rulers violated the purposes for which government had been established.

Property joined bodily self-ownership to productive labor and accumulation. The common good was increasingly identified with the protection of rights, security of possession, enforcement of contract, expansion of productive activity, and freedom to pursue private ends. Political society became less responsible for directing citizens toward a shared highest life and more responsible for maintaining the conditions under which individuals could seek happiness for themselves.

Rousseau then exposed the dependence, inequality, and artificial desires produced by this society. Human beings who appeared free had become dependent upon the opinions of others, and natural self-concern had been transformed into a restless need for recognition, distinction, and superiority. Property divided rich from poor, social comparison made esteem a necessity, and the desire to appear replaced the ability simply to be.

Society multiplied desires faster than it satisfied them and made individuals experience socially produced needs as their own. Rousseau placed the source of legitimate authority in a general will through which individuals were supposed to recover freedom by obeying laws they prescribed to themselves. But this required citizens capable of willing the common good rather than merely aggregating private interests. The liberation of the individual therefore demanded a new collective moral authority capable of transforming human beings into citizens.

Kant transformed Rousseau’s general will into the formal structure of morality. He separated the ought from the is because no observation of nature, desire, history, or custom could establish an unconditional duty. Freedom did not mean following inclination; it meant autonomy, the capacity of a rational being to give itself a universal law. A moral action was free because its principle could be willed as legislation for every rational being.

Natural right was consequently transformed into a system of equal external freedom. Each person was entitled to the greatest freedom compatible with the equal freedom of everyone else, not simply because self-preservation demanded it, but because every person had to be treated as an independent moral agent and never merely as a means. Legitimate political order became the public legal condition under which the freedom of each could coexist with the freedom of all.

Kant nevertheless left human beings divided between nature and freedom. As natural beings they belonged to a world of causes, desires, passions, and consequences; as moral beings they understood themselves as spontaneous authors of law. History might gradually produce a lawful civil and cosmopolitan order through conflict, ambition, and unsocial sociability, but lawful conduct was not the same as moral conduct. A well-designed constitution could make even a nation of devils behave peacefully if their competing interests were properly arranged.

History could therefore provide conditions favorable to freedom, but it could not prove that humanity was becoming morally better. Universal history remained a rational hope and moral postulate rather than theoretical knowledge. Freedom still stood beyond the historical world whose mechanisms human beings were learning to understand and administer.

Hegel attempted to overcome this division by showing that freedom did not stand outside history but became actual through it. Spirit was not an isolated mind confronting an external reality; it was the movement through which human beings produced institutions, confronted their contradictions, and came to recognize themselves in the world they had made. History became intelligible as the development of the consciousness of freedom.

In Hegel’s account, the ancient East had known that one was free, Greece and Rome that some were free, and the modern world that human beings as such were free. The passions, ambitions, conflicts, and suffering of individuals served this development without their fully understanding it. World-historical figures pursued their own purposes while advancing transformations demanded by their age, and reason used human passion to bring new forms of freedom into existence.

Christianity established the spiritual equality of souls, the Reformation brought inward freedom into the world, the Enlightenment universalized reason, and the French Revolution attempted to make abstract freedom politically actual. Yet freedom could not remain the abstract destruction of every inherited authority. When freedom recognized no institutions, distinctions, or enduring obligations, it could become terror—the attempt to destroy every particular reality that appeared to limit the universal will.

Freedom became concrete only through ethical life: the family, civil society, and the state. The person acquired rights through property, contract, occupation, recognition, and membership in a rational legal order. Civil society liberated private interest and productive activity but also generated poverty, dependence, and division, requiring institutions capable of educating particular interests toward the universal good.

The rational state was therefore not merely an instrument constructed by prepolitical individuals; it was the institutional form through which individuals could become concretely free. Hegel claimed to reconcile individual and community, freedom and necessity, reason and history, and the ought and the is. Yet this reconciliation depended upon the belief that history had reached a stage from which its preceding movement could be understood as a whole. Philosophy appeared only when a form of life had matured, and the owl of Minerva took flight at dusk.

Marx rejected Hegel’s reconciliation with the modern state because its proclamation of universal freedom and equality left the capitalist society beneath it untouched. Individuals were formally equal before the law while being divided by their relations to the means of production. Workers who did not own the conditions of their labor had to sell their labor power as a commodity, and the activity through which they transformed the world confronted them as a power belonging to someone else.

Political emancipation had created the rights-bearing individual while leaving human beings socially dependent, economically unequal, and alienated from their labor, products, one another, and their own capacities. The state appeared to represent a universal community while civil society remained organized by private property, competition, and class power.

Marx transferred the movement of history from spirit to material production. Human beings entered relations of production that shaped law, politics, religion, and philosophy, while changes in productive forces generated conflicts with existing property relations. Capital was not merely wealth owned by particular individuals; it was accumulated social power that returned against the people who had produced it.

Relations among human beings appeared as relations among commodities, products seemed to possess value and movement of their own, and the market concealed the human labor and domination embodied within it. The apparent freedom of exchange rested upon relations of dependence at the point of production, while the impersonal movement of capital compelled owners and workers alike to obey a system no individual controlled.

Capitalism nevertheless developed the productive powers required for liberation. It dissolved inherited orders, expanded human needs, united the world through production and exchange, and made possible an abundance beyond the limits of earlier societies. At the same time it concentrated ownership, intensified alienation, and created a proletariat whose liberation required abolition of the class structure itself.

Communism was intended to abolish private ownership of the means of production, permanent class domination, and eventually the coercive state. Human beings would consciously govern the productive powers that had previously ruled them, individual development would cease to conflict with the development of the community, and the realm of freedom would arise from humanity’s mastery of necessity. Yet Marx provided no final guarantee that people liberated from class domination would use their powers wisely. The conquest of nature could become the conquest of human nature, and the abolition of inherited authority could release powers for which no adequate standard remained.

Nietzsche then broke with the Hegelian and Marxian faith that history was a rational process moving toward reconciliation. He accepted that every form of thought was historical, but denied that history possessed an intelligible end capable of telling human beings what their mastery was for. The death of God meant more than the loss of religious belief: the sacred horizon that had ordered life had ceased to command, while nature, reason, and history had also lost their authority as universal moral standards.

Once every table of good and evil appeared as the creation of a people, age, religion, or human type, inherited values could no longer be experienced as necessary. Christianity’s moral inheritance continued to shape equality, compassion, and human dignity even as belief in the God who had authorized that morality disappeared. Modern humanity had destroyed the foundation while attempting to preserve the structure built upon it.

The freedom won from inherited values therefore opened into nihilism. Humanity possessed expanding scientific, economic, and political powers but no longer possessed an authoritative answer to the question of what those powers were for. The decisive problem ceased to be merely how human beings could be liberated and became who would create the ends toward which their freedom would be used.

Nietzsche called the activity by which life interprets the world, orders its drives, distinguishes higher from lower, imposes form, and creates values the will to power. This did not mean only the desire to dominate other people. It named the movement through which living beings overcame resistance, organized their conflicting impulses, fashioned identities, and gave meaning to a world that otherwise appeared as becoming without purpose.

The philosophers of the future would therefore be creators and legislators rather than workers who merely systematized inherited values. They would establish new horizons and determine the rank of human possibilities. Philosophy itself became implicated in the creation of the world within which human beings lived, and power now included the ability to determine the values through which desire, suffering, achievement, and failure were interpreted.

The death of the old order opened two opposing possibilities. The last man reduced freedom to comfortable self-preservation, health, safety, equality, consumption, entertainment, and petty pleasure. No shepherd remained because everyone belonged to the same herd; difference appeared as sickness, and the powers released by modernity were used to eliminate risk, suffering, longing, and greatness.

The overman represented the opposite possibility: a human being loyal to the earth, devoted to creation rather than comfort, and willing to treat humanity not as a finished end but as something to be overcome. Eternal recurrence was the severest test of affirmation. The creator had to will not only the future he hoped to make but also the past, suffering, chance, and mortality he could not undo, overcoming revenge against time by willing the whole of life again.

Yet Nietzsche’s attempt left a contradiction at the center of modern power. He denied a self-subsisting order of value while continuing to distinguish higher from lower, noble from base, health from sickness, and life-giving from destructive interpretations. The thinker who exposed every horizon as a creation was compelled to create a new horizon without being able to appeal to a standard beyond the act of creation itself.

After Nietzsche, historicism held that every form of thought arose within an epoch, language, people, and horizon whose presuppositions it could not fully escape. Nature was absorbed into history, and the Socratic distinction between what was good by nature and what was merely held by convention disappeared from view. Every doctrine could be explained through its historical situation, but historicism could not explain how its own claim to the historical character of every truth escaped the limitation it imposed upon all previous thought.

Positivist science simultaneously restricted genuine knowledge to observable facts, measurable regularities, and efficient causes. It declared value judgments incapable of rational proof and thereby increased human power while renouncing any authority to say how that power should be used or why science itself was worth pursuing. The choice of science appeared as groundless as every other ultimate commitment, and beneath the expanding structure of objective knowledge opened an abyss it could neither explain nor master.

Existentialism arose as the reaction of serious human beings to this relativism. From Kierkegaard it inherited the existing individual, anxiety, decision, and personal responsibility; from Nietzsche it inherited the death of God and the destruction of universally binding values. It removed Kierkegaard’s faith and attempted to free Nietzsche’s creative individual from every remaining appeal to nature or metaphysics.

Husserl had shown that the scientific world of neutral objects was derived from a prescientific world directly experienced by human beings. Heidegger radicalized this insight by arguing that things were encountered first through use, care, fear, need, and concern within a world in which human beings were already involved. The detached theoretical subject was not the beginning of human experience but a later possibility arising within an already meaningful world.

Human beings found themselves thrown into situations they had not chosen and formed themselves through projects whose ultimate justification could not come from a neutral standpoint outside existence. Meaning appeared to arise from commitments through which finite beings formed horizons and assumed responsibility for lives that would otherwise drift among the anonymous conventions of the crowd. Anxiety disclosed the collapse of familiar meanings, mortality disclosed the finitude of every project, and being-toward-death stripped away inherited assurances.

Authenticity meant accepting contingency and choosing resolutely without pretending that the choice rested upon objective certainty. Yet resoluteness could not by itself distinguish a just commitment from a monstrous one. If commitment created its own authority, existential freedom lacked a rational standard by which to distinguish philosophical courage from political frenzy, creation from destruction, or sacrificing oneself from sacrificing others.

Heidegger eventually moved beyond the individualist language of existentialism. The horizons within which human beings understood themselves were not simply projected by individual wills but disclosed through historical epochs of Being, each determining what could appear as real, true, useful, or possible. Philosophy ceased to seek a timeless rational order and attempted instead to hear what each historical disclosure revealed and concealed.

From this perspective, technological world society appeared as the completion of nihilism: a planet organized for production and consumption, work and recreation, security and satisfaction, in which rival political systems participated in the same project of rendering everything calculable, available, and controllable. Nature became a stock of resources, human beings became human resources, cultures were levelled into mass society, and individuals were gathered into a lonely crowd.

German nihilism turned revulsion against this future into irrational decision. Its original motive could arise from a genuine disgust with a world reduced to comfort, commerce, self-preservation, and entertainment; it became nihilistic when it could name nothing positively good for the sake of which modern civilization should be opposed. Violence, war, sacrifice, and obedience then appeared as proofs of seriousness, and destruction became preferable to an administered happiness emptied of nobility and purpose.

Strauss returned to Socrates because the crisis of modern reason could not be overcome by constructing another historical system or by choosing a new commitment without grounds. The return was not an attempt to restore ancient doctrines as unquestionable authority; it was a recovery of the questions those doctrines had kept open. What is justice? What is the good life? What is a human being? Is there a natural order by which cities, laws, and ways of life can be judged? Historicism had declared every answer historically conditioned, yet presented that judgment as a truth transcending history. Existentialism denied an objective ground for choice while continuing to prefer authenticity to conformity. Heidegger placed every form of thought within an epochal disclosure of Being while claiming to see the limitation governing the entire history of philosophy. Each position depended upon distinctions it could no longer rationally defend.

Historicism had revealed the succession of human caves, but it had not demonstrated that there was nothing beyond them. The Socratic image of the cave did not deny that human beings began within inherited languages, laws, myths, and opinions; it showed why those beginnings could not be treated as the final measure of truth. Socrates began from the authoritative opinions of the city and tested them through questioning, following their contradictions toward problems the city’s horizon could not resolve. Philosophy did not guarantee possession of a final answer, but it preserved the possibility of ascent—the possibility that justice and injustice, better and worse, noble and base were not merely creations of an age or decisions imposed by a victorious will.

The return to Socrates therefore led directly back to political philosophy. Political action always concerned preservation or change: preservation sought to prevent something from deteriorating, while change sought an order believed to be better. Both presupposed judgments about better and worse, even when those judgments remained concealed beneath the language of necessity, progress, security, or liberation. What political action preserved or changed was ultimately a regime—not merely a written constitution or a set of governmental offices, but an entire way of life. A regime joined particular rulers and their claims to rule with laws, institutions, property relations, education, authoritative opinions, honored human types, and an end toward which the community was directed. It determined not only who governed but what the society admired, what it rewarded, how it formed its citizens, and what possibilities it allowed them to recognize.

Another line of descent from Nietzsche led not back to Socrates but into psychology. Nietzsche had already treated conscious reasons as surface interpretations beneath which contending drives, instincts, cruelty, memory, guilt, and the desire for mastery continued to work. His genealogies asked what hidden form of life had produced a moral judgment, which forces spoke through an ideal, and what need was being served when a value presented itself as disinterested truth. The unity and rational transparency of the individual had therefore begun to dissolve before Freud gave that dissolution a systematic psychological form.

Freud transformed this suspicion of consciousness into an independent method for interpreting the psyche. Psychoanalysis separated itself from philosophy by no longer beginning with the question of what the soul ought to become or which way of life was good. It treated dreams, symptoms, slips, fantasies, compulsions, and neurotic conflicts as evidence from which the concealed causes of thought and action could be reconstructed. The conscious mind was not master in its own house: human beings could sincerely believe that they understood their motives while being driven by wishes, memories, defenses, and conflicts they had repressed or disguised. The human interior became a distinct field of investigation, diagnosis, and treatment, governed increasingly by its own concepts of psychic conflict, illness, adjustment, and health.

Freud argued that sexual and aggressive drives could not simply be released without threatening the cooperation upon which social life depended. Civilization required renunciation, sublimation, and an ego capable of mediating among instinct, internalized authority, and the demands of external reality.

Strauss did not answer Freud by choosing liberation over repression. He recovered the older question psychology had displaced: not merely how desire could be restrained, released, or adjusted, but how the passions could be educated and ordered toward a genuinely good life. The decisive distinction was not simply between instinct and repression, but between desire and choice, and between a disordered and a well-ordered soul.

Bernays recognized that this psychology could be used to govern mass society. Public relations linked products, leaders, and policies to unconscious wishes for status, security, sexuality, belonging, and self-expression. The object being sold mattered less than the emotional meaning attached to it.

Consumption became a means of organizing desire. Goods were presented not merely as useful objects but as expressions of identity, freedom, prestige, and personality. The citizen who might challenge concentrated economic and political authority could be reorganized as a consumer whose private satisfactions tied him to the existing order. The engineering of consent promised to make democracy manageable by directing irrational mass desires without appearing to abolish individual choice.

After the crises of mass politics, war, and totalitarianism, psychoanalytic institutions increasingly treated psychological adjustment as a defense of democratic order. A strong ego would control disruptive impulses, accept social reality, and fit the individual into family, work, and society. The well-adjusted personality became both a therapeutic ideal and a political type: independent enough to function, but stable enough not to threaten the order within which it functioned.

The regime of adjustment then provoked the revolt. Its first target was the Freudian claim that civilization depended upon controlling the dangerous forces within the mind. Wilhelm Reich reversed that claim. The unconscious was not originally a reservoir of destructive impulses that society had to restrain; its energies were fundamentally good, and it was their repression by family, morality, and authority that distorted them into sickness and aggression. Psychological health therefore seemed to require not stronger internal controls but the release of the self from the controls society had placed within it.

In the 1960s this therapeutic rebellion joined a political attack upon corporate society. Student radicals accused psychoanalysts, advertisers, and corporations of manufacturing conformist personalities whose identities were expressed through mass-produced goods. The policeman inside the mind appeared to have been placed there by institutions outside it. At first the conclusion was political: overthrow the state and corporations, and the internal authority they had created would fall with them.

But the American state proved far more powerful than the radicals had imagined. After the repression of the New Left, the attempt to transform society turned inward. If the external institutions could not be overthrown, individuals would enter their own minds, remove the controls implanted there, and create a new self from which a new society would eventually arise. Political liberation was displaced into personal transformation. Changing oneself no longer followed the transformation of the regime; it became the means by which the regime was supposed to be transformed.

The human-potential movement supplied the techniques for this inward revolution. At Esalen, encounter groups encouraged individuals to express feelings they had learned to suppress, abandon inherited roles, take ownership of their experience, and recover a true inner self supposedly hidden beneath social conditioning. Autonomy meant becoming the author of one’s feelings and identity. Yet when these techniques were applied to collective conflicts, they often dissolved the identities through which people had acted politically. Social antagonisms were translated into failures of personal openness, and the transformation of institutions receded behind the endless transformation of the self.

Werner Erhard carried the movement beyond the belief in a true self waiting to be released. EST stripped away one inherited identity after another until no fixed core remained. The emptiness beneath the social roles was presented not as a loss but as freedom, because from nothing the individual could invent himself and become whatever he chose. The search for an authentic nature within the person ended in the creation of a self without a given nature.

Erhard described this emptiness as existentialism’s endpoint. The connection was not a direct intellectual descent from Heidegger to encounter therapy, but a cultural enactment of the crisis Strauss had traced within modern thought. Positivism could explain causes while remaining unable to justify ultimate ends or even the choice of the scientific orientation itself. Existentialism began where that explanation ended, with the groundless freedom through which the individual formed a horizon and assumed responsibility for it. EST translated that philosophical movement into a popular technique of self-creation.

But the therapeutic form preserved the weakness Strauss had identified in the philosophical one. Authenticity described how a commitment was assumed, not whether its object was good. Resoluteness alone could not distinguish justice from fanaticism, self-knowledge from self-deception, or liberation from degradation. The individual was freed to create values, but the content of those values remained beyond rational judgment.

Curtis followed this problem into the world of desire. The revolt had overturned the Freudian suspicion of desire without recovering the older distinction between a desire and a good desire. Strauss insisted that desire was not identical with choice: a person could desire something while disapproving of the desire, resisting it, or despising himself for submitting to it. An impulse did not become a value merely because it was intense or experienced as one’s own. Psychology possessed methods for explaining and transforming the psyche, but its ends—health, adjustment, authenticity, fulfillment, and self-actualization—remained answers to philosophical questions its methods could not settle. The adapted personality and the expressive self appeared to be opposites, yet one received its standard from the existing order while the other transferred the creation of standards to the individual. Neither recovered the inquiry into the well-ordered soul or the common good.

This was the opening through which business absorbed the revolt. Market researchers discovered that the people who rejected conformity had not ceased to be consumers. They wanted commodities that expressed their individuality and difference. The problem was to identify these new desires without appearing to impose an identity from outside. The language of liberation supplied the solution: products could be presented as instruments through which individuals discovered, created, and displayed themselves.

The Stanford Research Institute transformed this insight into the Values and Lifestyles system. Drawing upon Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, researchers asked individuals to describe their inner values, aspirations, and images of themselves. The answers were processed into psychological types that crossed the older divisions of social class. The supposedly unique self turned out to express itself through recurring and measurable patterns. Individuality could be segmented into lifestyles, and each lifestyle could be connected to predictable products, images, and political messages.

The revolt against conformity thus generated a more flexible form of conformity. Corporations no longer had to manufacture one social character and sell the same goods to everyone. They could conform themselves to the new nonconformists, produce different commodities for different psychological types, and attach rebellion, spirituality, sexuality, ecological concern, authenticity, and freedom to purchasable ways of life. Changes in production made short runs and proliferating varieties profitable. A market once limited by finite material needs could expand through desires that changed whenever the self sought a new expression.

The individual felt liberated from social classification, yet his liberation had become the basis of a more intimate classification. He understood himself as an inner-directed person choosing his values; the corporation understood him as a member of a lifestyle group whose choices could be predicted. The positivistic method rejected as an account of the whole person returned as a technique for measuring the expressions of existential freedom. One language described a self that created meaning; the other translated that creation into data.

Reagan and Thatcher carried this individualism into politics by presenting freedom as the removal of government from personal choice and the satisfaction of wants through the market. Business increasingly assumed the task of reading and fulfilling individual desires, while citizens were encouraged to understand themselves as consumers whose private choices expressed their independence. The politics of liberation and the market of self-expression now reinforced one another. The generation that had rebelled against consumer conformity could embrace consumption because commodities appeared to help its members become themselves.

Politicians of the left adopted the techniques business had developed. Clinton’s Democrats and New Labour used focus groups, lifestyle research, and psychological segmentation to recover voters who no longer understood themselves through class or collective interest. Citizens were encouraged to speak about their fears, frustrations, aspirations, and private needs; policies and political language were then redesigned to reflect those feelings back to them. Politics became reactive. Instead of asking individuals to deliberate about purposes they shared, leaders treated their desires as demands to be discovered and satisfied.

This appeared to be a more democratic politics because experts were listening rather than commanding. The desires of the people had become powerful without the people themselves acquiring power. Citizens did not gain control over the institutions that shaped their lives. They were offered policies and images tailored to their feelings while corporations, market researchers, media organizations, and political professionals retained the power to define the alternatives. The active citizen was recast as a political consumer.

The change also disabled the collective power through which entrenched interests might have been challenged. Once voters appeared as shifting collections of private wants, political parties found it increasingly difficult to ask them to sacrifice an immediate benefit for a common purpose. Individuals could demand lower taxes and better public services simultaneously because focus-group politics registered each desire without requiring a judgment about their relation. Leadership surrendered the task of forming a coherent public purpose and returned to citizens an amplified image of their own contradictory wishes.

The decisive victory of business did not consist merely in persuading people to purchase particular commodities. It consisted in making the consumer model of the human being authoritative across society. Once politics accepted that its purpose was to discover and satisfy individual desires, it lost the language through which citizens might organize collectively against the power of business. Existing property relations and commanding institutions could remain largely untouched because politics had ceased to ask who ruled, toward what end, and whether the order itself was just.

The digital world extended this system beyond the history Curtis narrated. Desires no longer had to be inferred only from interviews, polls, questionnaires, or focus groups. They could be observed continuously through searches, purchases, movements, relationships, attention, and behavior. Systems could test which images produced fear, anger, desire, or attachment and alter the environment in response. The personalized world presented to each person appeared to arise from his own choices while those choices were being recorded, predicted, reinforced, and modified.

The movement was therefore not a simple succession in which positivistic psychology became existentialism and then became positivistic again. Two lines crossed. One treated the human being as an object produced by discoverable causes; the other treated him as a subject who created meaning through choice. The first could explain conduct but not determine its end. The second could affirm freedom but not judge the content of its commitments. Modern institutions joined the two by inviting individuals to experience themselves as creators of values while converting their expressions into lifestyles, preferences, predictions, and instruments of administration.

The older forces did not disappear within this transformation. The sacred survived in political myths and promises of redemption; the household in property, inheritance, and family authority; the polis in citizenship, education, and the desire for belonging; revelation in moral horizons that secular reason inherited but could not fully justify; sovereignty in states capable of law, war, administration, and surveillance; natural right in the rights of individuals; history in ideologies promising emancipation; and the conquest of nature in technologies capable of acting upon both the external world and the human interior.

These forces accumulated, collided, and changed form. Sacred order became ancestral law; ancestral law became the regime of the polis; the polis was judged by nature; nature was confronted by revelation; revelation was subordinated to sovereignty; sovereignty was authorized by natural rights; natural rights were transformed through autonomy and history; history was transformed into production and revolution; liberation opened into nihilism; and nihilism made the creation and administration of meaning into a political problem. Psychology entered this movement as a science that explained the self, a therapy that promised to liberate it, and a body of knowledge through which its liberation could be organized.

And so, in the age of the individual, the hopes, dreams, and uncertainties inside people’s minds became a driving force across the world. Institutions learned first to control individuals by strengthening the forces of adjustment within their minds and then, more powerfully, to govern by releasing, measuring, classifying, and feeding their desires. People came to experience the desires through which they were studied and directed as expressions of their own freedom. And the older powers that organized the world around them survived by making that search for freedom part of the machinery through which the modern regime preserved itself.
