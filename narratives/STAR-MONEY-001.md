# MONEY AND THE TRANSFORMATION OF POLITICAL ECONOMY

*From Property and Convention to Capital, Marginal Value, and Monetary Order*

Money did not become a decisive power merely because human beings invented a convenient instrument of exchange. It became decisive through a transformation in the place assigned to property, labor, acquisition, and production within the human whole. What began as a means serving households and cities gradually became part of an apparently autonomous order governed by its own laws, until Marx reconstructed that order as a historically specific system of social power. Jevons, Menger, and Walras then independently opened the post-classical period by rebuilding value around individual dependence upon goods at the margin. Jevons expressed the new theory through a calculus of choice and statical exchange; Menger reconstructed its causal foundations through needs, scarcity, production through time, and institutional emergence; Walras joined marginal valuation, money, production, saving, capital, and credit within a simultaneous system of general equilibrium.

In classical political philosophy, economic activity began from necessity. Human beings required food, shelter, tools, and the assistance of others, and no household could supply every necessary thing through its own labor. Differentiated needs therefore produced exchange, while money supplied a practical measure through which unlike goods could be made sufficiently commensurable. Yet neither exchange nor money determined the ends of human life. Acquisition served the household, the household belonged within the polis, and the polis existed not merely so that human beings could live but so that they could live well. Political judgment concerned justice, education, citizenship, and the common good, while philosophy asked what human beings and the political community ought ultimately to honor. Production remained subordinate to an order it had not produced.

The modern transformation began by changing the starting point. Locke placed the naturally separate individual and his rights before the particular political society in which he lived. Every person possessed a property in his own person, and the labor of that person supplied the original title through which something held in common could become private property. Appropriation was initially limited by use and spoilage: no one acquired a natural claim to take what would perish unused. Money altered that limit. Because durable money could be accepted by consent and exchanged for perishable surpluses, individuals could accumulate beyond immediate consumption without allowing what they had appropriated to spoil. The introduction of money therefore made durable inequality of possessions possible within Locke's account before government created that inequality.

Political society arose to preserve property in the broad sense of life, liberty, and estate through settled law, impartial judgment, and enforceable decisions. Economic activity nevertheless began to display regularities that government could not simply command. Locke's monetary writings distinguished the metallic content of money from its legal denomination. Calling a smaller quantity of silver by a larger name did not create the missing value; it altered contracts and redistributed claims between creditors and debtors. The market rate of interest likewise followed the supply of lendable funds, borrowing demand, commerce, and expected profit more powerfully than a legislature could determine it by decree. Political authority remained necessary to establish law and protect property, but economic relations increasingly appeared to possess a course of their own.

Hume reconstructed this order from a different foundation. Property did not exist in completed form as a natural title prior to society. Human beings were dependent upon one another, their generosity was limited, and the goods they desired were scarce and unstable. Under those conditions, each person could recognize an advantage in conventions that stabilized possession, provided others generally observed the same rules. Justice emerged through this common interest and repeated recognition. Transfer by consent allowed possessions to circulate, while promises extended cooperation beyond immediate exchange and created the basis of continuing credit. Government became necessary as scale, distance, partiality, and conflicting interests weakened the spontaneous force of convention.

Money served this expanding system without being identical to wealth. The wealth of a society consisted in its people, industry, skill, commodities, and capacity for production and exchange, not simply in its quantity of coin. An increase in money could stimulate activity temporarily as it moved unevenly through the economy, but continuing increases ultimately altered prices. International movements of specie also tended to reverse persistent trade imbalances by changing the relative prices of domestic and foreign goods. Low interest did not arise merely from abundant money; it accompanied developed commerce, accumulated stock, numerous lenders, and profitable employment. Public credit then extended promise into government. It allowed states to command present resources through claims upon future revenue, while creating permanent creditors, taxes, and political dependencies.

Smith joined these dispersed elements into the operating system of commercial society. The division of labor enormously increased productive power, but specialization required exchange. As the market expanded, individuals could devote themselves to narrower tasks because they could rely upon the products of strangers. Money arose from the inconveniences of barter as producers converged upon commodities that others would readily accept. It supplied the common instrument through which specialized dependence could be coordinated across a society whose members neither knew one another nor shared a single intention.

Accumulated stock made this system dynamic. Workers had to be supported before their products were completed and sold; tools, materials, buildings, inventories, and money had to be advanced; and portions of stock assumed different roles as consumption funds, fixed capital, and circulating capital. The price of the annual product was distributed among wages, profit, and rent, linking market exchange to the positions of workers, owners of stock, and landlords. Accumulation could enlarge the demand for labor and the productive capacity of society, while the very specialization that created wealth could narrow the worker's faculties and make public education necessary. Commercial society therefore never operated outside politics. Defense, justice, public works, education, taxation, and public administration remained conditions of the market order, while public debt enabled extraordinary expenditures to be separated from immediate taxation and converted into lasting claims upon public revenue.

Ricardo transformed Smith's historical and institutional account into a more rigorous system. The principal problem of political economy became the laws governing the distribution of the social product among landlords, capitalists, and laborers. Utility was necessary for a commodity to be exchangeable, but it did not measure exchange value. Relative value was regulated principally by the labor required for production, subject to modifications arising from the durability, composition, and turnover time of capital. Ricardo thereby separated wealth from value with unusual sharpness. Wealth meant an abundance of useful goods; value reflected the difficulty of producing them. Machinery could increase the quantity of goods and the productive capacity of society even while reducing the labor and value embodied in each unit.

The three class shares formed a single connected structure. Rent arose as cultivation extended from more advantageous land toward land requiring greater labor and capital, allowing superior land to yield a differential return. Rent was therefore an effect of high agricultural prices rather than their original cause. Wages possessed a market rate and a natural rate. The natural rate included the food, necessities, and customary conveniences required to sustain and reproduce the laboring population, and therefore varied across societies and historical habits rather than remaining a bare biological constant. Profits moved inversely to wages where other prices remained unchanged. As accumulation and population increased pressure upon the production of food, dearer necessities raised money wages, higher wages reduced profits, and rent increased. The system tended toward a stationary condition in which the motive for further accumulation weakened.

Agricultural improvement and imported food could postpone this tendency, giving free trade a direct relation to the distribution of class income and the continuation of accumulation. Comparative advantage showed that countries could gain through specialization even when one possessed an absolute productive advantage in every commodity, because capital moved less freely across national borders than within them. Money remained subordinate to these real relations. Metallic currency possessed a cost of production, while paper could replace an expensive monetary commodity and release capital for productive employment. But excessive paper issue depreciated the currency, so convertibility and institutional restraint were required. The gain created by replacing costly metal with paper accrued to whoever issued the paper, making currency design a question of distribution and political power as well as efficiency. Interest ultimately followed the return on capital rather than the mere quantity of currency or a rate declared by law.

Ricardo's treatment of machinery exposed a conflict within the classical promise of increasing wealth. Technological improvement could enlarge net revenue and reduce commodity prices while shrinking the gross fund that employed workers. Landlords and capitalists could benefit, national wealth could rise, and displaced laborers could nevertheless become redundant and poor. Greater productivity therefore did not guarantee that every class would share in the gain. Ricardo had converted political economy into a unified science of value and distribution, but the unity of the science disclosed opposed class positions within the wealth-producing system.

Marx inherited this completed architecture and turned it into an internal critique. The commodity joined a useful object to a social form of value. Concrete labors produced different things, but commodity exchange reduced those labors to abstract socially necessary labor and expressed their products through a common equivalent. Money did not enter from outside as a merely convenient token. It developed as the universal form in which the values of commodities could confront one another.

The difference between money and capital appeared in their circuits. When a commodity was sold for money so that another useful commodity could be purchased, money mediated consumption. When money was advanced to purchase commodities so that the result could be sold for more money, use became a moment within valorization. The purpose of the circuit was no longer a different object of consumption but an increase in value. Money had become capital.

The increase could not be explained simply by cheating in exchange, because exchange among legal proprietors appeared as the transfer of equivalents. Its possibility depended upon a peculiar commodity: labor-power. Workers were legally free to sell their capacity to labor, but their separation from independent access to the means of production made that sale necessary. Labor-power could be purchased at a value connected to the cost of its reproduction, while its use in production could create value beyond that amount. The excess supplied surplus value, which later appeared in distributed forms as profit, interest, and rent.

Capital was therefore not merely accumulated machines, materials, or money. It was self-expanding value embodied in a social relation and moving through successive forms. Money-capital purchased labor-power and means of production; productive capital joined them in production; commodity-capital carried the enlarged value back toward sale and renewed money. Turnover, fixed and circulating capital, and the reproduction of total social capital connected individual enterprises to a system no single capitalist controlled. Accumulation reproduced goods and productive capacity, but it also reproduced the separation between the owners of the means of production and workers dependent upon wages. Competition compelled individual capitals to reduce costs, reorganize labor, introduce machinery, and continue accumulation even when the aggregate consequences threatened particular participants or the stability of the whole.

The system acquired the appearance of a natural order. Relations among human beings appeared as relations among commodities, prices, wages, profits, interest rates, and movements of capital. Human beings created these forms through their activity, yet the forms confronted them as independent powers whose laws they had to obey. Commodity fetishism and alienation named this reversal. Capitalist society became a kind of social physics in which historically produced relations operated behind the backs of the producers as objective necessities.

This was the point at which political economy became an account of the whole social order. Relations of production appeared to explain classes, law, politics, ideology, and historical transformation, while industry became the practical relation through which humanity transformed nature and produced itself. Marx completed classical political economy by making its categories more historically and socially comprehensive, and he overturned it by denying that those categories were eternal. Property, wage labor, capital, and their economic laws belonged to a definite form of society that had been produced and could therefore be transformed.

Strauss accepted the force of Marx's diagnosis of alienation while questioning the sufficiency of its resolution. If productive relations explained every standard as the expression of a historical position, Marx still required a standard beyond that relativism to identify proletarian emancipation with human emancipation. If history possessed no natural or rational end, productive development alone could not guarantee that collective ownership and technological abundance would culminate in moral regeneration. The abolition of fixed division of labor and class rule could produce new requirements for expertise, administration, planning, and hierarchy. Freedom from oppression did not by itself guarantee the wise use of freedom, and the conquest of nature included a transformation of human nature whose end production could not judge.

Marx therefore closes the classical period at its highest point of expansion. Locke made the proprietor and consensual money central to enlarged accumulation. Hume made property, promise, credit, and government an order of coordinated conventions. Smith made exchange, division of labor, money, and stock the system of commercial society. Ricardo made value and class distribution the unified scientific problem of that system. Marx revealed the system itself as a historically produced form of social power organized around self-expanding value.

Money had moved from a practical measure serving exchange to the universal equivalent through which an entire society represented and reproduced its productive relations. Yet the completion of the classical theory also established its limit. Economics could reveal consequences, dependencies, class relations, and forms of compulsion that moral and political language concealed, but it could not supply its own ultimate answer to the question of what production, property, money, freedom, and political power ought to serve.

Jevons began the next period by declaring that the classical theory of value was not complete. Political economy would become a mathematical calculus of pleasure and pain because its central relations concerned quantities: quantities of commodities, varying intensities of want, increments of labor, and ratios of exchange. This was not merely the addition of equations to an unchanged science. It relocated the elementary problem. Ricardo had asked how the conditions of production regulated relative value and how the social product was distributed among wages, profit, and rent. Marx had made those relations the anatomy of a historical form of society. Jevons began instead from the relation between a person and an available quantity of a good.

Utility, in this reconstruction, was not an intrinsic substance residing in an object. The same thing could possess a high degree of utility under scarcity and almost none in abundance. Total utility therefore had to be distinguished from the utility of the last or next possible increment. Water could be indispensable in total and yet command little in exchange where the available supply had reduced the final degree of utility nearly to zero. As quantity increased, the urgency of the remaining want ordinarily diminished. The final degree of utility, rather than the total usefulness of the existing stock, became the point upon which economic choice turned.

Value was correspondingly reconstructed as a ratio of exchange rather than something deposited inside a commodity. Two trading bodies possessing different goods could increase their satisfaction by exchanging portions of their stocks. Exchange continued until an additional increment at the prevailing ratio would produce no further gain for either side. At that statical point, the exchange ratio corresponded inversely to the final degrees of utility of the quantities remaining after exchange. The market was rendered as a problem of allocation and equilibrium among marginal increments.

This did not make labor and production disappear. Jevons stated the intervening sequence with precision: labor affects supply; supply affects the final degree of utility; and the final degree of utility governs value, or the ratio of exchange. Labor therefore remained causally important without constituting a substance embodied in the commodity. It also entered the calculus from the other side as pain or disutility. Work continued so long as the expected utility of the additional product compensated for the burden of additional labor and stopped at the margin where the balance was lost.

The center of economic explanation had shifted. Classical political economy connected value to production and the distribution of a common product among social classes. Marginalism connected value to the choices of persons confronting quantities at the margin. The movement gained a more exact language for allocation, substitution, and exchange equilibrium, but its exactness depended upon bounding the problem. Jevons acknowledged that actual industry existed in perpetual movement and change; equilibrium was a statical construction used because the dynamic whole was harder to solve. Property titles, class formation, the historical production of wants, and the justice of the resulting allocation were not refuted. They were placed outside the immediate calculation.

Jevons's account of money showed why the economy could not, in practice, be reduced to the psychology of isolated exchange. Barter required a double coincidence: each party had to possess what the other wanted at the same moment and in exchangeable proportions. Money overcame this obstacle by serving as a generally acceptable intermediary and a common measure. It could also provide a standard for obligations deferred through time and a store or means of transmitting value. These functions were related but separable. Nothing required one material thing to perform all of them under every circumstance.

The monetary system therefore appeared as an arrangement of instruments with different legal and economic properties. Full-weight coin, token coin, convertible and inconvertible notes, cheques, bills, and other credit documents could each perform some work associated with payment without becoming identical things. A name could not abolish the differences among an immediately final payment, a redeemable promise, an order upon a bank, and a claim maturing in the future. Credit deferred settlement; it did not create real resources merely by multiplying documents that promised payment.

Banking nevertheless transformed how much exchange a given quantity of currency could support. Reciprocal claims could be entered as book credit and offset rather than separately discharged in coin. Cheques transferred claims upon bank deposits, while clearing houses brought the obligations of many banks together and canceled opposing debts. Money could remain the unit in which accounts were expressed even when no coin or note moved in the final settlement. A large part of exchange thus assumed the form of organized multilateral barter conducted through accounts, promises, and net balances.

The quantity of currency required by a society could no longer be inferred from the volume of trade alone. It also depended upon prices, the speed with which instruments circulated, the organization of banks, and the extent to which reciprocal obligations were canceled through clearing. A more efficient mechanism could conduct more transactions with less physical currency. Money had become not one object but an institutional architecture joining denomination, coinage, convertibility, credit, banking relationships, accounting, settlement, and law.

The marginalist turn therefore produced a double movement. In the theory of value, it narrowed political economy from Marx's historical account of production and social power to formally expressible relations among wants, quantities, and exchanges. In the theory of money, it disclosed an expanding institutional network without which those exchanges could not occur. The formal theory clarified how choices could be coordinated at the margin; the monetary mechanism revealed that coordination depended upon legal distinctions, trusted promises, organized intermediaries, and systems for extinguishing debt.

Jevons supplied one branch of the post-classical reconstruction, not a complete account of marginalism. He displaced labor from the direct determination of value without making production irrelevant, and he replaced the classical distributional center with individual choice and equilibrium without answering the political questions the classical system had exposed. Economics acquired a more precise account of how given wants and resources could be allocated, but that precision could not determine how wants were formed, whether property was rightful, whether a distribution was just, or what end the economy ought to serve. Marx had expanded economics until it claimed to explain the social whole; Jevons preserved economics as a narrower science by defining a more limited problem.

Menger reached the subjective theory of value in the same year by a different path. He did not begin from a calculus of pleasure and pain or from the equations of an exchange equilibrium. He began from causality. A thing became a good only where a human need existed, the thing possessed the capacity to contribute to its satisfaction, the person understood that connection, and the thing could be brought under human command. Goods of first order satisfied wants directly. Goods of second and higher orders contributed indirectly through sequences of production and could do so only in combination with the complementary goods required to complete those sequences.

Economic action was therefore a form of provision extending into the future. Human beings had to estimate which needs would arise, the quantities required to satisfy them, the goods already available, the technical means of production, and the time separating higher-order inputs from consumable products. Uncertainty entered at the foundation because future needs, harvests, production results, and available quantities could never be known with perfect precision. A developed economy did not escape time and ignorance; it lengthened the periods across which individuals attempted to provide for them.

Scarcity transformed useful goods into economic goods. Where the quantity available during the relevant period exceeded every anticipated requirement, command over a particular unit secured no satisfaction that would otherwise be lost. Where requirements exceeded the available quantity, some needs had to remain unsatisfied, and control over each unit acquired importance. Value was this recognized dependence of a person's satisfaction upon command over a concrete good. It was not a physical quality of the object or a substance that existed independently of the valuing person.

The magnitude of value followed from the ordering of dependent satisfactions. A stock of a good could serve more and less urgent uses. If one unit were lost, the person would ordinarily surrender the least important use still secured by the stock rather than sacrifice a more important one. The value of the unit was therefore governed by the least important satisfaction that depended upon it. This was marginal valuation without Jevons's differential calculus: a ranking of concrete needs and available units within the circumstances of a particular person.

Labor and cost could not reverse that direction of explanation. A thing did not acquire value because labor had been spent upon it, and the amount of past labor could not determine how much satisfaction now depended upon possessing it. A diamond found by accident could possess great value, while a product made through extensive labor could become valueless. Historical cost could reveal whether a completed act of production had been prudent, but it could not command the present value of its result.

Production nevertheless occupied a more explicit position in Menger than a simple opposition between utility and labor suggests. The anticipated value of goods of first order was transferred backward to the complementary goods of higher orders capable of producing them. Land, materials, tools, and labor services acquired value because control over them contributed to future goods upon which satisfactions were expected to depend. The causal direction ran from anticipated wants to consumer goods and from consumer goods backward through the productive structure. Because production required time, the valuation of an input depended upon the expected circumstances in which its product would eventually become available, not merely upon present conditions.

Exchange arose where this subjective structure differed between persons. One person possessed a quantity valued less than another good held by someone whose valuations ran in the opposite direction. If both recognized the opportunity and possessed the power to transfer their goods, exchange allowed each to provide more fully for his needs. Successive exchanges altered their stocks and therefore altered the marginal satisfactions dependent upon additional units. Exchange reached its economic limit when another transfer would cease increasing and begin reducing the satisfaction of one or both parties. Prices emerged within the limits established by unequal valuations and bargaining conditions; they were observable results of exchange, not objective quantities of value residing in the goods.

Menger then made the unequal saleability of commodities the bridge from exchange to money. A commodity could be easier or harder to sell according to the number of potential buyers, the geographical reach of demand, the quantity that could be marketed without a price concession, the time required to find a buyer, the costs of transport and storage, legal restrictions, and the organization of markets. Direct barter failed whenever a producer could not find someone who both wanted his product and possessed the exact goods he desired. But he could advance toward his end by accepting a more saleable commodity, even without wanting to consume it, because that commodity increased his chance of completing the next exchange.

As more individuals recognized this advantage, the most saleable commodities were accepted more frequently, and their wider acceptance made them still more saleable. Habit and imitation generalized a strategy that no one needed to design as a public institution. Money emerged from purposive individual exchanges whose cumulative result exceeded the intention of any participant. The state did not originally invent this monetary character. It later strengthened it by accepting the monetary good in public payments, enforcing its role in obligations, and standardizing metallic content through coinage.

This did not make money an exact measure of an objective exchange value. Actual purchases and sales occurred within ranges, and the price at which a holder could sell was not necessarily the price at which the same person could buy. No fixed quantity of money was therefore the objective equivalent of a commodity independently of persons, place, and time. Money nevertheless became the most practical instrument of price estimation because developed markets normally quoted and settled transactions in the most saleable commodity. It measured prices operationally without measuring a substance of value.

The Jevonsian and Mengerian constructions stand together without collapsing into one. Jevons treated economics as a mathematical calculus of marginal pleasure, pain, and statical equilibrium. Menger treated it as a causal-genetic science of needs, goods, scarcity, foresight, production, exchange, and institutional emergence. Both denied that labor or historical cost directly created value, but Jevons made labor operate through supply and final utility while Menger imputed anticipated consumer value backward through a time-structured process of production. Jevons decomposed an advanced monetary system into differentiated instruments and clearing mechanisms; Menger explained how a generally accepted medium could emerge before legislative design.

Together they shifted economics away from the classical organization of value around production and class distribution and toward the purposive allocation of scarce goods by individuals. They did not thereby refute Marx's historical questions. The theory of marginal value could explain choices made within a distribution of property without explaining how that distribution arose, whether command over goods was rightful, how social conditions formed the wants being ranked, or whether the resulting order served a defensible human good. Marginalism gained precision by defining a more limited field; Menger showed that even within that field, time, uncertainty, production, custom, and law could not be eliminated.

Walras opened a third route. He began by dividing what earlier writers had often joined under political economy. The pure science would explain exchange value; applied economics would ask how wealth could be produced usefully and abundantly; social economics would ask whether appropriation and distribution were just. Social wealth consisted of things that were useful and available only in limited quantity. This condition made them appropriable, exchangeable, and worth producing, but those consequences did not belong to one undifferentiated inquiry. Property raised a moral question, industry an applied question, and exchange value a pure scientific question.

Within the pure theory, Walras gave *rareté* a precise marginal meaning. Effective utility was the total intensity of the wants satisfied by a possessed quantity; *rareté* was the intensity of the last want that quantity satisfied. As the quantity held changed, the marginal want dependent upon it changed. Value was not an absolute substance residing in a good but a relation among goods, and equilibrium price ratios corresponded to ratios among their marginal *raretés*. Each trader reached maximum satisfaction when no further reallocation at the prevailing prices could improve the use of the goods he possessed.

Walras then moved from the individual condition to the market as a whole. A stationary price for two goods required total effective demand to equal total effective supply. With many goods, however, it was not enough to clear each pair separately. Every market had to clear at the same time, and every indirect rate of exchange had to agree with the relevant direct rate so that arbitrage through a third commodity could produce no advantage. Prices formed an interdependent structure: a change in one could not be understood without its relations to the others. *Tâtonnement* described the successive announcement and revision of prices—upward where demand exceeded supply and downward where supply exceeded demand—through which the system approached a consistent set of market-clearing ratios. Walras called the result an ideal state toward which competitive adjustments tended, not a condition that actual economic life continuously occupied.

The numéraire made this system expressible without supplying an absolute measure of value. Walras denied that any commodity possessed an invariant value comparable to an unchanging unit of length or weight. A chosen numéraire simply stated the prices of all other goods in relation to one common commodity. Money performed a different function. It mediated exchange by replacing a difficult direct barter with a sale for money followed by a purchase with money. The two functions could be distinguished even though Walras believed the same commodity should ordinarily perform both, allowing obligations to be paid in the very thing in which prices were stated.

Money was consequently a commodity, but not merely a commodity like every other. Its monetary use enlarged its utility, its divisibility and durability allowed it to pass between possession and successive consumption, and its general recognition made sale easier than it would be through barter. Precious metals approached these practical requirements because they were widely accepted, uniform, divisible, durable, portable, and relatively regular in value. Yet metallic transfer could itself be economized. Book credits, bills of exchange, banknotes, and cheques allowed reciprocal claims to be offset and settlements to be deferred or concentrated. Metal might disappear from a particular transaction while remaining virtually present in the numéraire, the obligation, and the possibility of final settlement.

Walras then extended the same architecture from exchange into production. Capital, in his terminology, comprised elements of social wealth that survived their first use and yielded successive services; revenue comprised elements consumed in the first service they rendered. Land, personal faculties, and produced capital supplied productive services, while the entrepreneur purchased those services and materials, combined them, and sold the resulting products. The market for productive services and the market for products were distinct but connected. Production equilibrium required demand to equal supply in both and the selling price of every product to equal its cost in productive services. Profit and loss drove entry, expansion, withdrawal, and contraction; at the ideal point of equilibrium, the entrepreneur as entrepreneur made neither profit nor loss.

Capital formation connected the present system to the future. Income not consumed or required to maintain existing produced capital could become saving. Credit transferred monetary savings to entrepreneurs who used them to acquire or create productive capital. The prices of capital goods depended upon their expected net services, while competitive buying and selling tended toward a common net rate of return. Capitalization was therefore not an isolated act of abstention. It was the systemic transformation of an excess of income over consumption into new productive goods, linking present wants, future production, monetary credit, capital values, and the structure of expected returns.

The three marginalist openings can now be distinguished at their proper levels. Jevons constructed a mathematical calculus of marginal pleasure, pain, labor, and statical exchange. Menger constructed a causal-genetic account of needs, goods, foresight, production through time, price formation, and the spontaneous emergence of money. Walras constructed a simultaneous system in which marginal valuation, every relative price, monetary mediation, productive services, entrepreneurship, saving, credit, and capital had to be determined in relation to one another. Jevons supplied the calculus, Menger the causal sequence, and Walras the systemic architecture; none can stand in for the other two.

Walras both narrowed economics and rebuilt an economic whole. He narrowed it by separating the exact theory of exchange value from the questions of useful production and just distribution. He rebuilt it by demonstrating that the supposedly narrow field was not a collection of isolated bargains but an interdependent order in which the conditions of every participant and every market entered the determination of the rest. This was not Marx's historical whole of class power and self-expanding value. It was a formal whole of mutually determined quantities and prices. Its precision depended upon taking wants, endowments, and rights of possession as data whose origin and legitimacy the equations did not decide.

The history of money therefore reaches a new threshold. Money began as a subordinate instrument of household and civic exchange; became the durable permission for accumulation, the carrier of convention and credit, the coordinating medium of commercial society, and the universal form of capital; then entered marginal economics as an emergent institution, a differentiated mechanism, and finally, alongside the numéraire, the practical mediation and common expression of a general equilibrium. The system could now show how individual wants, prices, production, saving, and credit were mutually coordinated with unprecedented exactness. It still could not determine whether the possessions from which exchange began were rightful, whether the wants being satisfied were good, whether the resulting distribution was just, or what purpose the power of the economic system ought ultimately to serve.
