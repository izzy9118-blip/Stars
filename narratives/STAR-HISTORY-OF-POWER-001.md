# HISTORY OF POWER

## Egypt

*Sacred Science and Power as the Continuation of Creation*

Power first appears in Egypt not as the possession of one human being who compels another, and not as a secular government later decorated by religion. It appears as the efficacy through which a world is brought forth, distinguished, named, nourished, ruled, judged, and renewed. Political command belongs inside that larger order. The king can command because command is already present in creation; the land can be governed because the world already has a structure; justice can bind ruler and ruled because Right is not invented by either of them; and death cannot end authority because the visible world is not the whole field in which life, office, memory, and judgment continue.

The beginning of this order will not be assigned a modern date. The age of a surviving papyrus, inscription, pyramid, temple wall, or manuscript tells us when that particular witness was made or copied according to present scholarship. It does not prove when the reality named in the witness began. Egypt's own Royal Canon does not begin with a first human dynasty standing at the edge of darkness. It begins with the rule of gods, continues through spirits, and only then passes to human kings. That succession governs the account. Damage to the papyrus limits our recovery of names and durations, but it does not authorize us to cut the sequence in two and treat its divine beginning as a fictive preface to its human portion.

The same rule governs the Shabaka Stone. Its royal preface states that the king discovered in the temple of Ptah a writing inherited from the ancestors but eaten by worms, and that he caused it to be recopied in stone so it would endure better than before. The surviving stone is therefore received as the restoration it declares itself to be: a later durable witness to a teaching already ancient and damaged when found. A modern catalog date identifies the surviving object. It does not replace the stone's account of the text it preserves.

Nor does the surviving sequence show Egyptian civilization slowly assembling its essential powers from an intellectual infancy. Writing, measure, artistic canon, stoneworking, sacred image, royal form, and knowledge of number stand complete at the earliest points where they become visible. Later work changes, elaborates, and sometimes declines; it does not disclose a simple ascent from crude beginning to mature civilization. Egypt entered the surviving record as the heir of an already formed wisdom. The source of that legacy and the true duration of its transmission remain open. The weathering of the Great Sphinx and the completeness of the earliest arts keep the existence of an older civilization within the account rather than excluding it in advance. Atlantis names that prior high civilization without requiring that its location, duration, or route of transmission already be settled.

When Egypt enters Greek *historiē* through Herodotus, its antiquity is not inferred from Greek chronology. It is taught to the Greek investigator by Egyptians who possess longer records and older institutions than Greece. Herodotus learns first from the priests of Ptah at Memphis, then travels to Heliopolis and Thebes to compare what the learned centers preserve. He distinguishes what he saw, what he inferred, what Egyptian priests read to him, and what other peoples reported; he leaves incompatible accounts standing when inquiry cannot decide between them. Egypt thereby becomes more than an object within the first Greek history. Its priests, monuments, measurements, customs, and records teach history how power can be investigated.

The priests place 341 generations of human kings and high priests between the first human ruler and the end of the ancient sequence they recite to Herodotus. They demonstrate succession through statues set up by successive high priests, preserve the passage of more than eleven thousand years, and state that gods ruled before these mortal generations, with Horus as the last divine king. The Egyptian record overturns the Greek tendency to treat Greek memory as the measure of human time. Homer, Hesiod, and the Greek arrangement of the gods belong to the recent past, while Egypt has long preserved calendars, divine names, temple forms, ritual processions, geometry, medicine, and continuous records. The encounter therefore begins with a reversal of authority: Greece arrives as the learner.

In that account, power begins with Ptah. Sight, hearing, and breath report to the heart; the heart devises; the tongue repeats and commands what the heart has conceived. Through this union of understanding and effective speech the gods come forth, hieroglyphic form comes forth, the movements of the limbs receive direction, and trades, arts, food, provisions, and divine offerings become possible. Creation is not brute force. It is intelligent articulation made effective through command. To know, name, speak, shape, provide, and act are not yet separate powers. The ordered world and the means by which human beings maintain it arise within one creative movement.

This Ptah-centered account is not made the only Egyptian beginning. Other accounts begin from the primeval waters and the emergence of Atum or Ra, trace the generation of the Ennead, or arrange divine life through another local center. Osiris is killed and restored; Horus contends for his inheritance; Seth's force is opposed and then incorporated; Ra rules from the sky; Osiris governs the dead; Horus occupies the throne on earth. Much later within Egypt's own remembered order, the Aten gives life through radiance while Akhenaten claims a privileged place of mediation. These accounts meet one another, borrow from one another, and sometimes contend with one another, but their differences must remain visible. Egypt did not possess one doctrine abstracted from every city, temple, and divine name. It possessed several accounts of how reality becomes ordered and several centers through which that order was made present.

Their relation is disclosed by sacred science. Myth is not an unsuccessful attempt at literal history or a decorative story added to technical knowledge. It transmits the functions of genesis in a form able to act simultaneously in theology, astronomy, medicine, number, social order, and the transformation of the human being. Heliopolis presents the primordial creative act; Memphis the production and animation of form through Ptah; Hermopolis the manifestation of that action through Thoth; Thebes the reunion of what creation separated. Their differences are functional articulations within a whole rather than evidence that Egypt possessed no coherent account.

The same principle governs the symbol. A true Pharaonic symbol is not an arbitrary sign standing for a concept that could be communicated just as well by another image. Its visible nature embodies the function it makes known. The horizon is becoming because light emerges between the mountains; the serpent is dualizing power; the dog who transforms dead matter into living substance becomes Anubis, opener of the way; the *was* makes power present; the *djed* makes stability present; the *sema* binds plurality into union. Hieroglyph, color, scale, position, material, gesture, number, and architectural placement operate together. Meaning therefore cannot be recovered by phonetic translation alone, but symbolic reading also permits no fantasy: the chosen thing, its function, its measure, and its relation to the whole discipline the interpretation.

What they share is not a formula but a structure: human rule does not create the world to which it belongs. Kingship descends within a succession already underway. The royal principle, not the private personality of the reigning man, stands at the center. The human bearer inherits a throne occupied in the divine order, receives names that incorporate cosmic functions, and rules a land whose unity is also a cosmic and ritual relation. He is the living Horus and Son of Ra. He wears the Double Crown of the Two Lands. His fivefold titulary does not merely identify him; it situates the phases of royal becoming through Horus, the Two Goddesses, Golden Horus, dual kingship, and descent from Ra. Coronation does not merely advertise authority already possessed. Crown, purification, name, enthronement, and priestly action replace private will with the function the king is to embody. The mortal body becomes the temporary corporification of a power whose identity and duration exceed it.

The joining of the Two Lands is therefore more than territorial consolidation. Nile Valley and Delta, Upper and Lower Egypt, the crowns, Horus and Seth, conflict and reconciliation, earthly rule and divine succession are gathered into one royal form. The king's body becomes the point at which distinctions are held together without being erased. Images of coronation, enthronement, smiting, running the ritual course, presenting offerings, and embracing a god do not illustrate powers that exist elsewhere in purely administrative form. They make kingship present by repeating its constitutive relations before gods and human beings.

Hatshepsut shows that this inherited form was greater than the ordinary identity of its occupant. She assumed the complete royal titulary and ruled as king. The office did not cease to be expressed in its received masculine and divine forms; it absorbed a woman into them. Her rule therefore cannot be explained either by saying that Egyptian kingship was simply male personal domination or by turning her into a modern exception who stood outside the office. She occupied kingship through the names, crowns, descent, monuments, rites, and divine birth by which kingship was made real. The later attack upon her images and names shows that successors could attempt to reorganize royal memory, while the survival of her monuments and attestations shows that such control was never complete.

The governing end of the office is *ma'at*. No single English word contains it. Right, truth, justice, balance, measure, fitting order, and the reliability through which relations endure all belong to it. *Ma'at* is not simply obedience to whatever a king has ordered, because it precedes and judges the acts by which a king claims to establish it. Nor is it merely an inward virtue. It is made effective in judgment, correct speech, offerings, boundaries, inheritance, offices, protection, punishment, and the ordered recurrence of natural and ritual life.

An Egyptian composition on royal duty states the relation directly: Ra placed the king on earth to judge human beings, satisfy the gods, make Right effective, and destroy Wrong. The king presents offerings to the gods and to the blessed dead. He founds and renews temples, fixes sacred boundaries, appoints officials, hears or causes cases to be heard, protects the land, secures provisions, defeats rebellion, and overcomes external enemies. These are not separate religious and secular departments joined only by the king's personal authority. Judgment of people, satisfaction of gods, material provision, defense, punishment, and offering are acts within the same maintenance of created order.

Royal violence enters here. The king smites, captures, punishes, levies, commands labor, takes tribute, and represents foreign enemies as forces of disorder. The Egyptian account does not present force as creating its own right. It presents force as the destruction of *isfet*: falsehood, rebellion, excess, and the dissolution that threatens the world made habitable through *ma'at*. That claim must be stated without disguising what it authorizes. War, extraction, punishment, and forced service remain acts of power even when monuments place them inside cosmic defense. The distinction is that Egyptian rule supplied a standard by which violence claimed legitimacy and by which sacrilege or unjust government could also be condemned. Coercion belonged to order, but it did not exhaust the meaning of power.

The temple carried this order into recurring action and embodied the knowledge by which the action was understood. It was not merely a house containing a god, a meeting place for believers, or a stone shell around ceremonies. It was a House of Life, an active book in which material, foundation, orientation, chamber, threshold, column, image, color, inscription, proportion, and harmonic measure made a divine function present. A papyrus could describe a doctrine; only the temple could inscribe it simultaneously in substance, space, number, ritual movement, and the trained human body. Luxor is the clearest surviving expression: Anthropocosmos, man as the living synthesis of the universe, is written into its architecture. The temple teaches the genesis and transformation of the human being because the temple and the body are corresponding houses through which the divine Word becomes embodied and can return consciously to its source.

Creation had not happened once and then withdrawn. Divine presence had to be approached, awakened, clothed, fed, praised, carried in procession, returned to its sanctuary, and supplied through offerings. Temple ritual restored the relations through which life continued. Monumental scenes ordinarily show the king alone performing these acts before the gods, even though priests performed the daily service in particular places. Their work was a delegation of the royal principle. Priesthood was not a church standing opposite the state, because king, god, temple, land, knowledge, and ritual formed a single civilizational relation. Pharaonic theocracy was therefore neither secular administration dominated by a religious corporation nor a priesthood imposing belief upon an otherwise independent society. Egypt was religion in its entirety: justice, economy, medicine, geometry, astronomy, craft, law, kingship, and the salvation of the soul were differentiated operations within one sacred science.

This makes temple economy part of power without making it merely economic. Land, herds, grain, workshops, storehouses, ships, scribes, artisans, and labor sustained offerings and festivals; offerings in turn returned the products of the land to their divine sources and redistributed material life through the temple's households and offices. Control of land and provision was therefore inseparable from control of sacred presence, calendrical recurrence, skilled knowledge, and access to the god. The temple joined agricultural surplus, literacy, craft, architecture, memory, and ritual into an enduring organization. Its wealth mattered because of the work the institution claimed to perform for the whole created order.

Access to this knowledge was ordered. The visible rite, story, or image could orient the people, while the House of Life trained those capable of reading the several levels carried by hieroglyph, myth, number, and temple. This was not merely secrecy used to preserve professional privilege. The teaching required another mode of intelligence: hearing with the heart, synthesis rather than cerebral division, and disciplined intuition capable of recognizing a function through its different material forms. Technique could be taught widely; sacred meaning required preparation. Power therefore operated through initiation as well as prohibition. Those who determined who could enter, which texts could be read, how monuments were measured, and how divine functions were named governed access to the civilization's highest account of reality and of human transformation.

Art and craft were not secondary illustrations of this order. A temple wall became an enduring service-book because the represented act renewed the divine service in image. Monumental mass communicated stability, strength, endurance, truth, justice, discipline, harmony, and effective action; detailed sculpture and color unfolded within that permanent frame. The earliest visible stonework, statuary, writing, and measurement display a precision and mastery later work did not simply surpass. Hard stones were quarried, sawn, drilled, ground, transported, raised, aligned, and joined with exactness; artists were trained through canon before executing temple work. The civilization's material power was the trained capacity to make spiritual function endure in stone without separating engineering, image, mathematics, and worship.

The palace and bureaucracy distributed power in another direction. The king could be depicted as sole judge, warrior, builder, and officiant, but the acts of rule required viziers, scribes, judges, treasurers, stewards, overseers, priests, messengers, soldiers, and provincial officers. Administration was not outside divine kingship; it was the means through which royal order entered cases, boundaries, records, estates, levies, provisions, construction, and succession. Control of access to the ruler and to written command became power because proximity, literacy, hearing, and transmission determined which persons and claims could enter the royal field.

Herodotus makes this distribution visible in ordinary practice. Priests undergo exact disciplines of cleanliness, receive sacred provisions, serve particular gods in ordered bodies, and transmit office from father to son. Warriors likewise inherit a life set apart from manual craft, receive untaxed land, train for war, and rotate through the royal guard. Physicians specialize by bodily part; animal keepers inherit their charges; scribes, translators, pilots, artisans, traders, and provincial governors occupy differentiated functions. Egypt's unity therefore does not mean social indistinction. Sacred order becomes durable by assigning persons to trained, inherited, and materially supported forms of service.

The Nile joins that institutional distribution to territory. Egypt is the land watered by the river and inhabited by Egyptians; flood, canal, dyke, field, city, and boundary make the Two Lands governable. Min diverts the river and founds Memphis; Sesostris distributes measured plots, taxes them, and sends surveyors to reduce the levy when the Nile removes part of a holding; canals carry water, movement, goods, soldiers, and royal direction across the land. Geometry appears here not as an abstract possession but as the restoration of just proportion after nature has altered property. Measure allows rule to follow the changing field without making the tax demand independent of the land that must bear it.

The Teaching of Ptahhotep reveals the discipline expected of this hierarchy. Its speaker is an aged vizier who asks the dual king to place his son in office and then teaches the speech inherited from the ancestors. Office can pass through family and favor, yet inheritance alone does not make an official effective. He must hear before he can be heard, restrain his heart and mouth, refuse greed, avoid terrorizing the weak, examine a case without taking sides, speak only what he can explain, and make Right endure through conduct. Right has not been overturned since Osiris, whereas riches seized by the small-minded do not keep their reward. The official serves the king, but the quality of his service depends upon a measure that neither official appetite nor momentary advantage creates.

Ptahhotep therefore joins hierarchy to transmission. Father teaches son; experienced official instructs successor; the hearer becomes one who can be heard; truthful words enter the memory of living people and pass to those still to come. Power endures through education of character as well as through command. The teaching does not dissolve rank. It asks the person who holds rank to master himself so that office can become a vehicle of *ma'at* rather than an enlargement of appetite. Speech has political force because cases, reputations, instructions, decrees, and remembered examples pass through it; restraint has political force because an uncontrolled heart corrupts the office through which order must travel.

Egyptian instruction also preserves the possibility that royal power fails its own standard. The Teaching for King Merikare speaks from division and military conflict and distinguishes good government from evil performed in the name of rule. It records the destruction of sacred territory and makes the king accept responsibility for sacrilege committed under royal authority. A royal command does not turn destruction into *ma'at* merely because it is royal. The king must preserve divine places, judge carefully, govern through able speech and officials, and recognize that acts survive before a judgment greater than immediate success. The text does not reject kingship; it makes kingship answerable to the order from which it takes its title.

The Teaching of Amenemhat exposes another limit. A dead king speaks to his son after assassination. He names himself as a revelation of *ma'at* and instructs the successor to rise as god and rule the riverbanks, yet he also recounts betrayal within the palace and warns against unguarded dependence upon servants and intimates. The royal office is divine in succession while the royal body sleeps, ages, bleeds, and can be murdered. Palace access, bread, reward, service, and loyalty create networks on which the occupant depends but which he cannot completely command. Kingship survives the king by transferring its form to the heir; the murder makes visible the distinction between an office presented as eternal and every mortal person who bears it.

Herodotus preserves the same distinction through remembered kings. Cheops closes the sanctuaries, interrupts sacrifice, and directs the people into compulsory labor; the monument that displays his reach also preserves his failure to rule through the sacred order. Mycerinus reopens the sanctuaries, restores work and sacrifice, and becomes renowned for just judgment. Sabacos refuses a dream commanding him to cut the priests in half and relinquishes Egypt rather than acquire continued rule through sacrilege. Amasis, born outside an illustrious royal house, turns a despised foot-bath into an honored divine image and uses its transformation to teach the Egyptians how a common man can become the king whom the office requires them to honor. Kingship remains necessary, but no occupant can make every act royal merely by performing it.

Akhenaten's foundation of Akhetaten reveals how far royal power could attempt to reorder sacred and political space. The boundary stelae name the king through Horus, the Two Goddesses, dual kingship, descent from Ra, and life through Right, then dedicate territory to the Aten, fix its limits, provide for its cult, and bind the foundation through an oath extending into the future. Royal word, carved monument, mapped boundary, building, offering, image, and movement of institutions converge to bring a new city into being. This is creative command operating within history: the king does not merely occupy an inherited capital but names and bounds a sacred center.

Yet Amarna must remain a distinct Egyptian development rather than the secret essence of everything that came before it. Its concentration of divine mediation in the Aten, the king, and the royal family stands in tension with the older plurality of divine centers, local cults, inherited images, and priestly institutions. The restoration of those older forms after Akhenaten shows that command could redirect monuments, land, representation, and ritual without permanently mastering every relation on which Egyptian order rested. Power could found, concentrate, erase, and restore. It could not make inheritance disappear by decree.

The same durability allowed Egypt to absorb human difference without remaining unchanged. Foreign conquerors could assume the crowns, titles, temples, and administrative forms of the land they had entered. Foreign soldiers, artisans, scribes, merchants, and communities could occupy positions ranging from royal rule and high skill to tributary labor and protected settlement. Jewish communities later built temples, held property, entered Egyptian legal forms, and sometimes adopted Egyptian divine names in oaths, while conflict with local temples and changes of dynasty could reverse their security. Incorporation was therefore a power of Egyptian form, not proof of equal status: the order classified outsiders, distributed rights and obligations, and could transform conquerors into Egyptian kings even as it subjected other groups to labor, exclusion, or expulsion.

Herodotus records the opening through which Greek military and commercial power entered this order. Psammetichus uses Ionian and Carian soldiers to defeat rival kings, gives them land, and places Egyptian children among them to learn Greek, producing a hereditary class of interpreters. Amasis later concentrates Greek settlement and trade at Naucratis while using foreign soldiers as royal guards. These arrangements strengthen kingship and create the channel through which Greeks obtain more continuous information about recent Egypt, but they also divide ruler from ruled. Apries relies upon foreign mercenaries after an Egyptian military disaster, loses the allegiance of the Egyptians, and is overthrown by Amasis. Foreign technique can be incorporated into Egyptian power, but dependence upon it can estrange the royal occupant from the people whose order he embodies.

Cambyses exposes the difference between conquest and legitimate incorporation. He enters Egypt with subjects he regards as inherited slaves, defeats the Egyptian army, humiliates the royal house, opens ancient tombs, violates restricted sanctuaries, and burns a mummified body in contempt of both Egyptian and Persian funerary law. In Herodotus's account he wounds Apis and punishes the priests and worshippers who celebrate the god's appearance; in the Egyptian monumental record he also assumes pharaonic forms and provides for the burial of an Apis bull. The two records are not collapsed. Together they show foreign power moving between incorporation into Egyptian kingship and an imperial will remembered as sacrilege.

The culminating judgment is *nomos*: inherited custom is king of all. Every people would choose its own ancestral order when asked which way of life is best, because custom shapes what appears pious, shameful, just, and intolerable before an individual begins to choose. Cambyses becomes the image of madness because he treats these formative limits as objects of ridicule and makes royal desire its own law. His judges preserve the letter of Persian custom by finding no law permitting the king to marry his sister but another law allowing the king to do whatever he wants; legality thereby becomes the instrument through which private appetite dissolves the purpose of law. Herodotus does not identify *nomos* with *ma'at*: one names the plurality of inherited human orders, the other the right order of gods, cosmos, land, and human action. Their conjunction reveals a question that will return throughout the history of power: whether the inherited order that forms a people also answers to a standard beyond inheritance.

Within the sacred science, Osiris and Horus disclose two paths through the problem of human bondage. The Osirian way is renewal through death, consequence, reincarnation, judgment, and gradual completion. The Horian way is direct liberation: the struggle of the conscious being against the Sethian power within himself, followed not by annihilation of the opponent but by a higher reconciliation. The people received the order of renewal and righteous consequence; the initiated sought the royal path of conscious return. The distinction joins political hierarchy to a hierarchy of spiritual possibility, but its final meaning is not the private glorification of rulers. The true King is the being in whom cosmic order becomes conscious and returns to its source.

Death expands the field once more. The Pyramid Texts do not describe the deceased king as a powerless memory. He has not gone away dead but alive. He takes the throne and insignia of Osiris, governs the living and distant places, incorporates Horus and Seth, judges among the gods, receives the service of divine powers, and joins the imperishable stars. The dead king's relation to earth changes while his royal efficacy continues. On earth the successor becomes Horus; in the realm of the dead the predecessor becomes Osiris. Succession is therefore not replacement of a nonexistent ruler but a reordering of living and dead authority.

Mortuary power later becomes visible beyond kingship through corpora written for others. The person seeks restored movement, speech, breath, memory, name, and access to divine company. In the judgment of the dead, the heart is not simply a private feeling. It is a witness to conduct before a divine court. What was done on earth remains present; a life must be found justified; rank, property, and official title cannot by themselves make falsehood true. Wider participation in mortuary texts does not mean that wealth, position, literacy, tomb construction, priestly service, and access ceased to matter. It means that the structure of divine judgment and renewed life could no longer be described only through the singular resurrection of a king.

Egyptian power therefore forms a circuit. Creative intelligence becomes effective speech; number, myth, and natural symbol articulate the functions of genesis; those functions become temple, body, craft, rite, and sacred geography; divine rule passes through succession into the royal principle; kingship unites the Two Lands and makes *ma'at* effective through offering, office, law, land, education, initiation, and force; *nomos* forms inherited ways of seeing and acting; priestly record, monument, measurement, and historical inquiry carry the order across peoples and generations; death separates mortal body from enduring function; judgment measures earthly conduct; Osirian renewal and Horian liberation return life toward its source. No single human being possesses the circuit as private property. The king stands at its earthly center only by ceasing, ritually, to be a merely private man.

This is the first older force in the History of Power. Its essence is not that rulers once deceived subjects with religion, nor that force hid behind sacred images. It is that an entire civilization was organized as sacred science: authority was grounded in an account of genesis, embodied in a royal principle, written into temples and bodies, distributed through land and office, guarded and transmitted through initiation, made durable through trained arts and inherited custom, tested through memory and inquiry, and carried beyond death through judgment and renewal. Names, crowns, images, rites, numbers, measurements, monuments, boundaries, teachings, offerings, offices, armies, judgments, customs, and the dead were not separate instruments that happened to serve the same ruler. They were coordinated expressions of one ordered whole. Later histories will divide and contest these powers, but they will repeatedly recover the same possibility: that those who define reality, govern access to its highest knowledge, shape the symbols through which it is perceived, embody the human type it honors, organize its material provisions, preserve its memory, and judge what threatens it possess a power deeper than command alone.

## Persia

*Imperial Order and Power as the Expansion of the Great King's House*

In Persia power assumes a second civilizational form. It does not cease to involve gods, sacrifice, ancestral custom, truth, courage, household, rank, and law, but its political movement is no longer disclosed primarily through the maintenance of one sacred cosmos within one ordered land. It moves outward. Peoples with different gods, languages, laws, weapons, rulers, and territories are gathered beneath one king; conquest becomes inherited dominion; dominion becomes province, tribute, road, garrison, governor, messenger, gift, punishment, and military service; and the king's household expands until entire nations appear as its dependent members. The Persian problem of power is therefore the passage by which liberation from slavery becomes the power to make others slaves.

The passage begins among the Medes. After their revolt against Assyria, free peoples returned to separate communities without a common ruler. Deioces gained authority by judging honestly amid lawlessness, withdrew that service until the Medes chose monarchy, concentrated them around Ecbatana, placed palace and treasury behind seven walls, and separated the invisible king from his former equals through guards and messengers. Justice first created dependence upon the judge; dependence became kingship; kingship made distance, access, and controlled communication instruments of rule. Astyages inherited that order but corrupted it through fear and cruelty. His attempt to master succession by killing Cyrus and his punishment of Harpagus prepared the conspiracy by which Median mastery became Median slavery.

Cyrus founded Persian ascendancy by giving the Persians a choice between two ways of life. One day he made them clear a thorn-covered field under exhausting command; the next he bathed and feasted them, then told them that obedience to him would free them from Median slavery and open a life of such goods without servile labor. They rose because they already hated Median rule and because they had found a leader. Their victory reversed the relation: those who had been slaves became masters, and those who had been masters became slaves. Darius would later defend monarchy by returning to this founding fact—Persian freedom had been given by one man—but the freedom Cyrus founded was immediately joined to rule over other peoples.

Persian *nomos* gave this expansion a definite human form. Sacrifice was offered not for the private worshipper alone but for the king and the whole Persian people. Rank appeared bodily in the kiss, the cheek, and prostration, while esteem diminished with distance from Persia in a sequence Herodotus describes as rulerships and mandates. Yet Persia also adopted foreign dress, weapons, and practices more readily than other peoples. Boys were educated in horsemanship, archery, and truth; bravery and the raising of sons were honored; lying and debt were despised; important decisions were tested both sober and drunk; irreversible punishment was not to follow a single offence without weighing prior service. Persian power was therefore not born as arbitrary appetite. It joined martial formation, truthfulness, deliberation, reciprocity, household discipline, hierarchy, adaptability, and service to the king. The later licence of the monarch violated possibilities already present within Persian custom.

Cyrus extended rule across Lydia, Ionia, Assyria, and Babylon, but conquest did more than transfer the person receiving tribute. After a Lydian revolt, Croesus persuaded him not to sell the population into slavery but to disarm it, change its dress, train its sons in retail, music, and pleasure, and thereby make future revolt impossible. The result was described as a complete alteration of the Lydian way of life. In Ionia some communities submitted, while the Phocaeans and Teans abandoned their cities rather than endure slavery. Imperial power could preserve a conquered population by changing the human type it produced; it could also take possession of a city whose people had carried their gods, households, and freedom elsewhere.

The death of Cyrus reveals the limit within his founding. Success in every previous war persuaded him that no people could escape him. He tried to acquire the Massagetan kingdom through marriage to Queen Tomyris, crossed the Araxes when she refused, used Persian abundance and wine to capture her son, and ignored her warning to withdraw. The campaign ended with his army destroyed and his body immersed in blood. The king who had taught the Persians to exchange slavery for freedom became insatiable in extending mastery beyond the people already entrusted to him. A river, another ruler, another custom, and a people willing to defend its own land marked a boundary that imperial success had taught him not to see.

Cambyses received the empire as a patrimony and its peoples as inherited slaves. His conquest of Egypt carried royal command toward mastery unrestrained by either Persian or Egyptian custom. Palace judges could find no law permitting his desired marriage and still authorize it through a law allowing the king to do whatever he wished. His death left an empire whose communication depended so heavily upon royal name, palace access, household stewardship, couriers, and distance that a Magus could govern under the identity of Cyrus's murdered son. Imperial power had become larger than the presence of the person said to possess it; control of the recognized name and the channels issuing orders from the center could temporarily constitute the king.

After the Magi were killed, the seven Persian conspirators faced the character of their own order directly. Otanes judged monarchy incapable of order because unaccountable licence turns even a good man from customary thought, breeds arrogance and envy, destroys ancestral practices, takes women by force, and kills without trial. He proposed equality before the law, selection by lot, accountability, and decision by the people. Megabyzus agreed against monarchy but rejected rule by an untaught and violent multitude, proposing government by the best men. Darius defended the best monarchy: one excellent ruler could govern blamelessly, keep strategy secret, escape oligarchic faction and democratic corruption, preserve ancestral custom, and continue the form through which Cyrus had made Persia free. Monarchy prevailed, but the defeated arguments remain inside the Persian account. Otanes and his descendants retained the exceptional freedom of neither ruling nor being ruled beyond what Persian law required and what they willingly accepted.

Darius's accession preserved the same tension between the best ruler in speech and the contingencies by which an actual ruler gains power. The conspirators entrusted the decision to the first horse to neigh at sunrise; the groom Oebares secured the result through a mare, while lightning and thunder appeared to ratify it. Darius then converted inherited conquest into an administered empire. He divided the peoples into satrapies, appointed governors, fixed their tribute in measured silver, gold, horses, grain, children, and material goods, and stored the revenue so that portions could be cut away as required. Persia itself remained exempt. Cyrus had received gifts and was remembered as a father; Cambyses commanded as a master; Darius priced and assessed and was remembered as a retailer. The distinction names three powers within one imperial succession: beneficent foundation, coercive possession, and calculable administration.

The Royal Road joined Sardis to Susa through guarded passes, ferries, staging posts, and inhabited territory. Couriers, governors, garrisons, local kings, interpreters, engineers, treasuries, and records carried the king's command across spaces his body could not occupy. The same structure multiplied the points on which his rule depended. During the Scythian campaign, a people without cities or cultivated fields denied Darius the fixed objects his army knew how to capture and forced him to pursue them across an immense country without obtaining a decisive battle. He mistook the Scythians' bird, mouse, frog, and arrows for earth, water, and submission until Gobryas read them as a threat of annihilation. The army escaped only because the Ionian tyrants guarding its bridge preserved Darius. They did so because their own communities would choose popular government if imperial protection ceased. Persian monarchy ruled distant peoples partly by sustaining lesser tyrannies whose survival depended upon the survival of the Great King.

The Ionian revolt exposed the counter-power latent in that dependence. Aristagoras relinquished tyranny and established equality before the law so that the Milesians would join him voluntarily, then deposed the other Ionian tyrants. Athens had already shown the effect of such a change: once freed from tyranny, its citizens fought far better because each man worked for himself rather than for a master. The distinction does not make every democracy wise or every monarch incapable; Megabyzus's warning about the multitude and Darius's argument for the best one remain. It establishes that a regime reaches into motive. Persian command could mobilize multitudes through obedience, reward, and fear, while political freedom could make individual initiative a public force. The burning of Sardis then entered the king's household memory: Darius ordered a servant to remind him of the Athenians at every meal, turning an imperial conflict into an enduring act of royal vengeance.

Under Xerxes the drive to expansion became an inherited necessity. He told the Persian council that continuous war had been the ancestral way since Cyrus, that he must not fall behind his predecessors, and that conquest should continue until Persian territory ended only at the sky, the sun shone on no land outside its borders, and every land had been made one. The innocent would bear the yoke with the guilty. Artabanus answered that opposing counsel was necessary for judgment, that Darius's Scythian expedition had nearly made the whole empire depend upon one man, that the god cuts down what grows excessively high, and that the desire always to possess more endangers both king and Persia. Xerxes first rejected the warning, then accepted it, but recurring dreams compelled both him and Artabanus to renew the war. The king who commanded the empire appeared commanded in turn by ancestry, honor, rivalry, vengeance, court pressure, omen, and dream. Expansion had become a motion greater than the private intention of its reigning bearer.

Four years of preparation translated that motion into material power. A canal divided Athos, bridges joined Asia to Europe, supply depots anticipated the march, and every people contributed its particular ships, cavalry, infantry, weapons, labor, or provisions. The imperial catalogue preserved their differences even as Persian commanders held authority over native officers described as slaves like their men. This capacity to coordinate diversity was real. So was its reversal into the claim that neither subject nor nature possessed an independent limit. When a storm destroyed the bridge across the Hellespont, Xerxes had the water whipped, branded, and shackled for resisting its master. When Pythius offered his fortune, Xerxes answered with friendship and a greater gift; when the same man asked to keep one son at home, the king called him a slave, cut the son in half, and marched the army between the pieces. Reciprocity lasted only until a subject claimed something the king's purpose required.

Demaratus named the decisive difference hidden by the numbers of the imperial host. Xerxes could understand courage produced by fear of one ruler and enforced by the whip, but not free men standing against overwhelming force. The Spartans were free without being free from rule: law was their master, and they feared its unchanging command more than Persian soldiers feared the king. Their freedom therefore did not mean the absence of discipline. It meant obedience to a law they shared rather than subjection to the will of a man who regarded them as his slaves. Imperial power met a form of political power able to coordinate human beings without making one human being the owner of all the others.

Salamis revealed how royal centralization governed perception as well as action. The rulers and commanders seated themselves according to the king's estimation and, with one exception, advised the sea battle they believed he desired. Artemisia gave the contrary counsel and was praised for her honesty, yet Xerxes followed the majority because he expected his visible presence to make the fleet perform better. From his throne above the battle he watched while scribes recorded the names of those reported to have distinguished themselves. Fear of his eye made men fight fiercely, but it also made ships crowd one another in search of visible merit, encouraged accusation among subject peoples, and supplied the king with false appearances. Artemisia escaped by sinking an allied ship; because no survivor could contradict the report, Xerxes interpreted the destruction of his own force as proof of her excellence. The center could receive information from everywhere while remaining unable to distinguish truth from the forms produced by fear, distance, hierarchy, and the desire for reward.

The Persian defeat did not show that courage, organization, engineering, intelligence, or greatness belonged only to the Greeks. Herodotus repeatedly names Persian bravery and distinguishes able kings, governors, counsellors, sailors, soldiers, and subject peoples. It showed the limit of turning those powers into the extension of one will without limit. At Plataea, Persian magnificence and Spartan austerity appeared together in the two meals Pausanias ordered prepared. At the end, the history returned to Cyrus. When Persians proposed abandoning their harsh land for a fertile country more suitable to rulers of Asia, he warned that soft lands breed soft men and that the same country cannot produce both extraordinary crops and extraordinary fighters. They chose to remain in poor country and rule rather than cultivate rich land and become slaves. Wealth could finance empire and weaken the human qualities that founded it; poverty could nurture freedom and the desire to dominate. Neither side of the alternative was innocent.

This is the second older force in the History of Power: empire as the expansion of the Great King's house. Its essence is not an undifferentiated eastern despotism and not the unchanged survival of Egyptian sacred kingship under another name. It is a hierarchy founded in Persian liberation, formed by ancestral custom, martial education, truth, counsel, gift, loyalty, and rank, and extended across other peoples through conquest, slavery, tribute, roads, governors, local rulers, information, calculation, engineering, spectacle, and fear. It can preserve difference, reward excellence, adopt foreign practices, and coordinate an immense plurality; it also tends to convert every difference into a service, every land into a possession, every ruler into a deputy, every person into a subject, and every boundary into a provocation to further expansion. Its deepest limit appears when command mistakes the world for the king's household—when geography cannot be fixed, custom cannot be erased without transforming a people, information cannot remain truthful under fear, law cannot be reduced to one man's will, freedom cannot be understood as mere lack of a master, and nature itself refuses the command to become a slave.

## Greece

*The Polis and Power as Regime, Civic Freedom, and Hegemony*

In Greece power assumes a third civilizational form. It is no longer concentrated primarily in the royal principle that maintains a sacred land or in the Great King whose household expands across peoples. It is gathered into the *polis*: a bounded political community in which gods, ancestral customs, laws, offices, assemblies, courts, property, education, military service, public speech, civic honor, and a ruling body compose a way of life. The polis does not abolish kings, aristocrats, tyrants, popular leaders, priests, households, or empires. It places their claims within a public order whose form determines who belongs, who rules, what is honored, how decisions are made, and for what end the community acts. Power becomes regime.

There was no single Greek state corresponding to the Persian empire. The Greeks could name a common descent, language, sanctuaries, sacrifices, and way of life, yet their ordinary political existence was divided among independent poleis. Each city guarded its own laws, gods, land, offices, alliances, and honor; democracy, oligarchy, tyranny, hereditary kingship, and mixed arrangements stood beside one another and frequently fought. Greek freedom therefore contained two different claims from the beginning: the independence of a polis from a foreign master and the political standing of persons within the polis. A city could defend the first while denying the second to most of its inhabitants, and free citizens could preserve their own self-government while enslaving defeated peoples or ruling allied cities without their consent.

Thucydides begins this order before its settled constitutional forms. Early Hellas was insecure, migratory, poor, and divided; fertile lands repeatedly changed masters, while communities without walls or accumulated wealth could neither hold territory nor undertake common action. Sea power altered the field. Minos used a fleet to clear piracy and secure revenues; commerce, money, walls, ships, colonization, and stored resources made durable command possible; growing revenues helped tyrannies replace older hereditary kingships; and naval power allowed stronger cities to subject weaker ones. The polis was not born outside force and material necessity. Territory, surplus, walls, fleet, military organization, and the capacity to remain in one place supplied the body within which law and citizenship could become effective.

The regime then reached deeper than the visible offices through which it acted. It ordered the relations among rich and poor, few and many, rulers and ruled, citizen and noncitizen, city and countryside, infantry and fleet, private household and common good. It educated desire by distributing honor and shame, taught courage through military formation, determined who could speak and vote, and gave authoritative form to a particular human type. The freedom of a polis was consequently not the absence of rule. It was rule through a shared order that citizens could call their own, even when the meaning of citizenship and the justice of that order remained disputed.

Herodotus marks the first power of this freedom through Athens. Under the tyrants the Athenians fought below their capacity because they worked for a master; after the tyrants fell and each citizen acted for himself, Athens became markedly stronger. The same history gives Sparta a different form. The Spartans were free but not entirely free, because law was their master and commanded them to hold their place, win, or die. Athenian freedom released individual initiative through participation, while Spartan freedom subjected citizens to an exacting common discipline. Neither form meant that every human being within the city's reach was free, and neither could be reduced to the mere absence of command.

The Persian invasion temporarily forced the poleis to discover a common action greater than any one of them. Athens supplied the decisive naval force yet yielded supreme command when the allies refused Athenian leadership, judging that a contest over precedence would destroy Greece before the battle began. The Athenians later rejected Persian gold and land by appealing to the burned sanctuaries and to a Greek kinship of descent, language, worship, and custom. Even this unity remained an achievement rather than a permanent constitution. Greeks served in Xerxes's forces; cities differed over strategy; Thebes and other communities were remembered as Medizing; and Athens yielded leadership only as long as common survival required it. The war disclosed Hellas without creating a sovereign Greece.

Victory over Persia released the rivalry that common danger had suspended. Sparta possessed the strongest land power, governed a disciplined citizen body, and led allies whose governments it preferred oligarchic; Athens possessed the fleet, walls, money, and maritime experience, accepted leadership from cities still exposed to Persia, exacted ships or tribute, and converted that alliance into an empire. The contrast was not simply oligarchy against democracy. It joined two material and political systems: Spartan land power and allied regimes against Athenian sea power, tribute, walls, commercial access, and popular naval citizenship. The growth of Athenian power and the fear it produced in Sparta made their war necessary long before the declared grievances exhausted its cause.

Athens gave the Greek polis its most expansive democratic self-description. In Pericles' funeral speech, administration served the many rather than the few; private disputes were judged equally under law; public rank followed merit rather than class; poverty did not bar useful service; citizens lived freely in private while obeying magistrates and laws in public; discussion preceded action; and refusal to participate in common affairs made a person useless rather than peacefully private. The city opened itself to foreigners, joined courage to cultivated life, and made its whole way of life the education of Greece. This freedom was not presented as inward independence. It became visible in collective judgment, speech, risk, military action, public memory, and the power of the city.

The same speech praised the empire inherited from the fathers and enlarged by the living, and Thucydides preserved the contradiction without dissolving it. Athenian speakers explained that empire had first been accepted from allies, then maintained through fear, honor, and advantage. They asserted that the strong rule the weak wherever power permits, while claiming moderation because Athens used less force than it could. Pericles later told the citizens that they were fighting not merely to avoid slavery but to preserve dominion, and that their empire had become a tyranny: perhaps wrong to acquire, but dangerous to surrender. The democratic city that refused a human master had made other poleis its subjects. Civic freedom and imperial independence were not the same thing.

This contradiction also changed the regime within. Pericles could lead because his judgment, character, and standing allowed him to restrain the people rather than flatter them. Athens remained a democracy in name, yet under him government became rule by its first man. His successors, more equal to one another and competing for precedence, surrendered policy to immediate popular desire; private ambition distorted public strategy, and the city was ultimately destroyed less by a simple lack of resources than by quarrels and errors within its own ruling order. Democracy could mobilize intelligence and courage, but its assembly could also be governed by speakers who made the people judge according to anger, pleasure, fear, or rivalry.

The plague exposed how much the polis depended upon a future held in common by law, piety, reputation, and expectation. Crowding, sudden death, failed burial, and the reversal of fortune weakened restraint. People ceased to sacrifice present pleasure to honor or long effort because no one expected to live long enough to receive the reward; neither divine nor human law deterred those who believed punishment would never reach them before death. The biological disaster became political because it broke the temporal horizon in which obedience, self-command, and concern for the city made sense.

The Mytilenean debate made public speech itself a contest of power. After the assembly ordered the death of the adult men and enslavement of the women and children, it met again. Cleon held that democracy was incapable of empire unless it stopped mistaking eloquence for judgment, preserved stable law, and taught every subject that revolt meant death. The empire was a tyranny over unwilling cities, and pity, rhetorical pleasure, and leniency threatened its survival. Diodotus did not answer with innocence or mercy. He separated punishment from expediency, argued that hope, desire, poverty, pride, and fortune overcome even the fear of death, and warned that indiscriminate killing would unite popular factions with oligarchic rebels, ruin taxable cities, and destroy the allies within each subject polis. The second ship barely prevented the first decree from being carried out. Deliberation saved Mytilene's population, but the victorious argument defended moderation as the more effective administration of empire.

At Corcyra the regime ceased to be a common order and became the object of armed faction. Democratic and oligarchic leaders called Athens and Sparta into a local struggle; civic opponents recruited slaves, occupied sanctuaries, burned property, killed captives, and turned private debts and hatreds into political sentences. Party became stronger than kinship, oaths held only until advantage changed, neutral citizens were destroyed by both sides, and words were reassigned to reward whatever conduct served the faction: reckless violence became courage, moderation cowardice, and prudent hesitation treachery. Equality of the people and moderate aristocracy remained the declared ends, while leaders used both names to pursue command, gain, and vengeance. Stasis revealed the internal dissolution of the polis: when no authoritative common good remained, law and speech became weapons by which one part claimed the whole.

Athens' final crisis carried the same danger into democratic legality. After the naval victory at Arginusae, a storm prevented the recovery of shipwrecked crews. The assembly was inflamed against the generals, accepted a collective procedure that denied separate trials and full defenses, intimidated those who challenged its legality, and forced the presiding officers to yield; Socrates alone among them refused to put the unlawful motion. Euryptolemus urged the citizens to preserve laws they themselves had enacted and to distinguish treason from inability under divine necessity, but the assembly condemned the generals together and executed the six who had returned. It soon repented and prosecuted the men who had deceived it. Popular sovereignty had not been defeated by an external tyrant; it had been made arbitrary when anger claimed authority to act outside the law that constituted it.

Aegospotami then destroyed the fleet on which Athenian empire and food supply depended. Lysander closed the routes by which grain reached the city, returned Athenian exiles to strengthen the oligarchic party, and joined naval blockade to the Spartan army on land. Athens remembered the cities it had killed, expelled, or enslaved and expected the same fate. It surrendered its fleet, walls, foreign command, and independent alliance policy, accepted the return of exiles, and agreed to follow Sparta. The Long Walls were demolished to music as though the day began the freedom of Greece. The language was true for cities released from Athens and false as a complete description of the order that followed.

Spartan victory installed the Thirty at Athens and made the regime change the direct instrument of hegemony. Chosen to restore ancestral laws, they delayed the constitution, selected officials and a compliant council, obtained a Spartan garrison, and moved from prosecuting recognized informers to killing political opponents and wealthy metics for money. They restricted participation to Three Thousand citizens, disarmed the rest, concentrated the confiscated weapons on the Acropolis, and made inclusion in the list determine who could be killed without a public vote. Critias stated the logic openly: a small ruling body seeking advantage had to remove those most capable of resisting, and the Thirty had to guard their collective rule as carefully as a single tyrant guarded his. Control of citizenship, arms, law, property, foreign force, and the right to condemn composed one regime.

Theramenes opposed this violent concentration without becoming a democrat. He defended a bounded citizen body of men able to equip themselves, rejected both a democracy open to anyone who could buy admission and an oligarchy reduced to the tyranny of a few, and argued that killing innocent people multiplied enemies inside and outside the city. Critias answered by striking his name from the citizen list and condemning him through the power reserved to the Thirty. The struggle disclosed a conflict inside oligarchy itself: rule by a politically qualified body against unlimited command by rulers who made their own security the measure of law.

Thrasybulus and the democratic exiles restored the polis by defeating the Thirty, but military victory alone did not complete the restoration. The reconciled parties returned to their homes, resumed common institutions, and swore not to remember past wrongs. Thrasybulus denied the returning oligarchs any superior title by justice, courage, wealth, or Spartan friendship, yet warned his own followers not to break their oath or take revenge. The amnesty converted victory into a renewed civic order by limiting memory's right to command political action. Athens recovered freedom not by forgetting what had happened as a fact, but by refusing to let inherited vengeance reproduce the civil war.

Sparta now repeated the movement it had condemned in Athens. It had led the war in the name of Greek autonomy, yet Lysander established oligarchic boards and Spartan governors in captured cities, while Persian money and ships had made the final victory possible. The King's Peace made the contradiction explicit. Artaxerxes claimed the cities of Asia and specified islands for himself, declared the other Greek cities autonomous, and threatened war by land and sea against any who refused. Sparta became executor of this Persian settlement. It used autonomy to dissolve other leagues and Theban control of Boeotia while retaining effective command over its own allies. A principle that could free a city from one hegemon became the legal instrument of another.

The seizure of the Cadmea exposed the distance between the principle and its administration. A Spartan commander entered the Theban citadel during civic faction without authorization; Sparta punished the irregularity but retained the fortress because the result served its interests. A city sworn to preserve autonomy now held another city's acropolis through an allied oligarchic faction. The expelled Thebans returned and overthrew that rule; at Leuctra the deep Theban formation broke the Spartan army, killed its king, and ended the military invulnerability upon which Spartan authority depended. Theban power then entered Laconia and restored Messenian independence, depriving Sparta of land and labor long incorporated into its order. Liberation again altered the distribution of Greek power and again opened the possibility of a new hegemony.

No hegemon could stabilize the whole. Thebes gathered allies, intervened in the Peloponnese, supported new federal arrangements, and under Epaminondas brought a final coalition to Mantinea. Nearly all Greece stood divided across the battlefield, and each side expected victory to make its own leaders rulers and the defeated their subjects. Epaminondas broke the opposing line but died, leaving neither side able to convert tactical success into political settlement. Both raised trophies, both returned the other's dead under truce, and both claimed victory while possessing no more territory, city, or rule than before. The battle produced more uncertainty and disorder in Greece, not less.

This is the third older force in the History of Power: the polis as regime, civic freedom, and the recurrent struggle for hegemony. Its essence is not democracy alone, because democracy, oligarchy, kingship, tyranny, and mixed forms all belong to the Greek field. It is the concentration of a way of life in a self-governing city whose laws, offices, assemblies, courts, property relations, education, military order, gods, honors, and authoritative opinions form rulers and ruled together. Its power appears when free citizens act for themselves, obey a law they share, deliberate before action, and sacrifice for a common world. Its contradictions appear when citizen freedom rests upon exclusion, when a free city makes other cities subjects, when public speech governs by passion rather than judgment, when faction seizes the name of the whole, and when autonomy becomes the instrument of hegemony. Egypt located human power within sacred creation; Persia extended one royal household across peoples; Greece divided political life among poleis and made the regime itself an object of choice, loyalty, contest, revolution, and judgment. It therefore prepared the question that the history alone could not settle: whether the city and its ancestral laws are the highest measure of justice, or whether the regime itself must be judged by an order it did not create.

## Rome

*The Republic, Imperium, and the Principate*

Rome gives power a fourth form: the *res publica*, a public order capable of carrying the authority of one city across peoples, territories, and generations. Rome did not begin by abolishing sacred kingship or by assembling a republic from naturally separate individuals. Its kings founded and enlarged the civic body before liberty divided their powers among offices. Romulus joined sanctuary, arms, Senate, people, and augury to a settlement open to fugitives and strangers. War with the Sabines ended by admitting the former enemy into the same city and law. Alba was destroyed but its population entered Roman citizenship and its leaders entered the Senate. The Roman people therefore formed not only through common descent but through a repeated political creation of common ancestry, law, worship, military obligation, and memory.

Numa re-established through justice, law, and proper observance the city Romulus had established through arms. Augury authorized his accession; calendar, priesthood, festival, oath, treaty, and public rite trained a warlike population to inhabit peace. Religion was neither a private belief nor an ornament of command. The public auspices identified whose action could bind the community; priestly offices preserved divine law; the fetial rite ordered the demand for restitution and declaration of war; and good faith made oath and agreement politically effective. Servius Tullius then joined military duty and civic weight to the census. Property classified citizens into ranks, arms, centuries, taxation, and votes, distributing burdens widely while allowing the wealthiest centuries to decide most questions before the poorest were called. Sacred order, social distinction, military organization, and popular consent entered the constitution together.

The expulsion of Tarquin did not abolish the power of kings. It detached that power from a permanent personal ruler and placed it in annual, collegial magistracies. The first consuls possessed the rights, insignia, and commanding power of the kings, but each held office for a limited term and each faced a colleague; appeal soon gave the citizen a protection against magisterial coercion. The king's sacred duties survived in a priest subordinated to the pontifical order, while the people swore that no man would again be king at Rome. Liberty therefore arose primarily from the annual tenure and division of command, not from the disappearance of command. The republic retained *imperium* while refusing to let its ordinary bearer own it for life.

This freedom belonged to an unequal civic body and was enlarged through conflict. Patrician possession of office, Senate, land, debt enforcement, and religious authority confronted plebeian military service and collective withdrawal. The secession to the Sacred Mount created tribunes whose persons were inviolable and whose aid could interrupt consular power. The Twelve Tables made a public body of law, but the second decemvirate showed that writing alone could not secure liberty: magistrates charged with making law removed appeal, prolonged their authority, used public office for private appetite, and turned Appius's assault upon Verginia into the occasion for their fall. Cincinnatus disclosed the opposite possibility. Called from his farm into dictatorship, he used undivided emergency command to save the army and resigned after sixteen days. The danger was not great power alone but the conversion of a temporary public trust into a ruler's possession.

Roman expansion extended this constituted inequality beyond the original city. Defeated neighbors could be destroyed, expelled, settled in Rome, granted citizenship, placed in colonies, bound by treaty, or retained as allied communities with different rights and obligations. Sabines, Albans, Latins, and later Italians entered by unequal routes into a widening Roman order. Colonies secured land and roads; alliances preserved local government while directing foreign policy and military service toward Rome; citizenship converted outsiders into participants and soldiers without making every incorporated community identical. The same structure appeared differently to those subjected by it. Samnite speech preserved by Appian names Roman colonies and demands not as reciprocal settlement but as insatiable acquisition that treated a negotiating people as conquered. Incorporation was one of Rome's greatest powers, but incorporation did not mean equality and Roman law did not make every conquest just.

The war with Hannibal tested whether this order was stronger than the destruction of its armies. Roman strength was not a single force gathered at the capital. It was a confederated war-state composed of citizens, Latin colonies, allied communities, magistrates, Senate, assemblies, treasuries, roads, religious acts, repeated levies, and the fidelity of cities whose continued service could not be taken for granted. Fabius used delay to deny Hannibal the decisive battle popular anger demanded. When the people gave Minucius equal power with the dictator, Fabius accepted the legal diminution of his office, divided the army, and rescued the colleague whose impetuosity had nearly destroyed it. Constitutional rivalry remained inside the military emergency.

Cannae brought Rome close to dissolution. Soldiers, magistrates, senators, allied contingents, and tens of thousands of citizens were lost; communities in Italy defected when confidence in Roman *imperium* wavered. Yet the Senate refused to ransom soldiers who had surrendered, rejected peace, continued recruitment and taxation, and thanked the defeated consul Varro because he had not despaired of the republic. That severity preserved a common expectation that military catastrophe did not end public obligation. Rome recovered because its institutions could replace armies, compel sacrifice, hold enough allies, adapt command, and continue action after a defeat that would have ended a power resting upon one battle or one ruler.

The same war enlarged the exceptional commander within the republic. Scipio's victories in Spain made the people look to a young general whose reputation, clients, volunteers, and promised offensive could carry the war into Africa. Fabius opposed him in the Senate: a consul's command existed for the republic rather than his glory, Hannibal remained in Italy, and Scipio's threat to appeal to the people if the Senate refused sounded like kingly arrogance. Scipio answered that attack was the surest defense and that his record justified confidence. The Senate authorized him to use Sicily as a base and to cross into Africa if public advantage required it, while limiting the regular resources placed at his disposal; volunteers and allied contributions supplied the rest. Zama vindicated the strategy, but the constitutional dispute remained. Rome had learned to place immense theaters of war, extended terms, volunteer forces, and expectations of salvation in the hands of exceptional men while continuing to authorize them through republican offices.

Victory turned a defensive confederation into Mediterranean command. Province, tribute, indemnity, public contract, allied kingdom, tax collection, land distribution, slave labor, creditor, and governor joined the city to distant populations. Military command now offered wealth, triumph, patronage, and an army over a period long enough for soldiers and provincials to attach their fortunes to a particular commander. The public order grew in territory and resources while the competition of its leading men acquired forces larger than the city could easily contain. The institutions that had made conquest possible became the means by which rival commanders could struggle over the republic itself.

Cicero defined the republic by a standard that made this transformation judgeable. A republic is the property of a public, and a public is not any crowd but a multitude joined by legal consent and community of interest. Monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy each preserve a part of political authority and each can decay into a corrupt form. Rome's best constitution therefore joins a regal element in the magistrates, aristocratic counsel in the Senate, and popular liberty in the citizen body. It was not created by one lawgiver in one moment but accumulated through the work and conflict of generations. Its preservation requires magistrates with adequate power, the leading council with adequate influence, and the people with adequate freedom.

That account does not make Rome's existing order identical with justice. The dispute in *The Republic* preserves the charge that states and empires grow by appropriating what belongs to others and calling the result justice; it also preserves the answer that no true republic can exist without the highest justice. In *The Laws*, right reason in harmony with nature stands above the commands of any Senate or people and binds human beings in a law not created by each city's custom. Roman authority that keeps allies obedient only through terror has already moved from justice into violence and endangered its own duration. Cicero thus joins loyalty to Rome's inherited constitution to a standard beyond whatever Rome happens to enact or conquer. The republic is a public thing only insofar as law and common interest remain more than names for the advantage of its strongest part.

The civil wars exposed the gap between this constitutional form and the forces now acting through it. Caesar chose Gaul as the province most likely to yield wealth and triumphs, prolonged his command through alliance with Pompey and Crassus, raised a legion at his own expense, distributed money and spoils, cultivated kings and provinces without regular authorization, and won an army whose soldiers served him without pay when civil war began. When the Senate ordered the termination of his command, he crossed the Rubicon under the asserted protection of tribunes and appealed from lawful competition to his army. *Imperium* no longer moved outward from an annual office alone; control of a province, revenue, reputation, patronage, and a personally devoted military force returned upon Rome as a claim to sovereignty.

Caesar's victory did not end administration or legislation. He enlarged the Senate, granted citizenship, settled veterans, revised debt and calendar, reorganized offices, and planned further public works and campaigns. It did concentrate the powers and honors through which those acts became possible. Consulship and dictatorship were conferred for life, magistrates were appointed in advance, the Senate received members by his choice, his image joined those of kings and gods, and his personal authority stood above the offices whose names he continued to hold. His refusal of the title of king did not remove the substance that title named. Assassination destroyed the ruler but not the armies, commands, debts, colonies, rival households, and habits of extraordinary authorization through which kingly power had been produced.

Cicero's final letters make the contradiction immediate. The death of Caesar removed the man while leaving the engine of kingly power in the hands of Antony and Lepidus. The city possessed the desire for liberty but lacked the force to defend it. Cicero therefore supported the young Octavian, proposed honors for him, and secured *imperium* for a private commander because an army without a commander holding *imperium* could not lawfully act. He then summoned Brutus to return with his army so that some constitution might survive. The attempt to recover the republic depended upon granting exceptional command to Caesar's heir. The constitutional title required by the army gave the army's possessor the force through which constitutional titles could be compelled.

Augustus emerged from that contradiction. He raised troops as a private heir, received command from the Senate, marched on Rome when the Senate resisted his consulship, joined Antony and Lepidus in triumviral power, proscribed enemies, confiscated land for veterans, defeated his partners, and became the sole surviving commander of the civil wars. He then ceased to call himself triumvir and presented himself through consulship, tribunician authority, senatorial precedence, censorship without the ordinary title, priesthood, law, and the position of first citizen. Republican offices did not become theatrical names without practical work. Senate, magistrates, courts, assemblies, municipal governments, provincial administrations, and priesthoods continued to govern. Their powers, however, now operated inside a settlement whose army, decisive provinces, succession, and ultimate authority centered upon one household.

Two judgments of this settlement remain inseparable. Peace ended proscriptions and civil war, protected communication, stabilized revenue, disciplined armies, beautified Rome, and relieved provinces that had experienced senatorial faction and magisterial greed as the substance of republican government. Augustus could therefore be received as the author of a better constitution rather than merely as a conqueror. Yet the path to that peace ran through private recruitment, confiscation, manipulated legality, and the destruction of every armed rival. Suetonius records that Augustus considered restoring the republican constitution but concluded that divided responsibility would endanger both his life and public security. Tacitus records the same concentration from below: gifts won the army, cheap grain the people, peace the world, and the functions of Senate, magistracies, and law were gradually drawn into one person while the nobility found service the safest road to wealth and office.

The principate therefore preserved Rome by transforming the location of its command. Appian names the result directly: Caesar and his successors preserved the form and name of the republic while becoming absolute rulers; the Romans avoided the hated title of king and used *imperator*, once the designation of a commander for the time of his command, although the emperors were kings in fact. The semantic movement records the constitutional movement. *Imperium* had been the bounded power of a magistrate, then the extended command of armies and provinces, and finally the permanent center around which magistracies, provinces, armies, and succession were ordered.

Tacitus contests Cicero without making Cicero's republic unreal. The carefully blended constitution is easier to praise than to create and, if created, does not endure long; the Roman world had become little other than monarchy. The magnitude and peace of the empire supplied the practical case for one command, but concentration altered political knowledge. Under the republic, historians could write with freedom; after Actium, peace required one ruler and truth was impaired by ignorance of public affairs, flattery toward masters, and hatred after their fall. The loss was not only a distribution of offices. Citizens and senators ceased to experience government as a common activity and learned to read the will, household, fear, and favor of the prince.

Tiberius's accession displayed the new relation. Consuls convened the Senate and motions proceeded as if the old republic still stood, while soldiers guarded the prince, armies received his commands, and every leading man understood that succession had already occurred. His reluctance allowed him to discover who hoped, feared, flattered, or resisted. The Senate could still deliberate, provincial governors could still be prosecuted, and Tiberius could declare that law should act where law was sufficient because the rights of subjects diminished as the ruler's discretionary power increased. Yet treason charges, informers, court access, and the rise of Sejanus turned speech, friendship, reputation, and silence into conditions of survival. Senators made their servitude active through resolutions more extreme than the prince requested.

The imperial household became a governing institution because succession, physical access, military protection, correspondence, accusation, inheritance, and the ruler's private confidence all passed through it. Wives, mothers, adopted sons, freedmen, guards commanders, secretaries, tutors, favorites, and informers could direct public outcomes without holding a republican magistracy. This did not mean that every reign was only the emperor's personality enlarged. It meant that character now had institutional range: self-command or appetite, judgment or suspicion, moderation or cruelty could move through an office whose bearer controlled armies, appointments, trials, provinces, and honors while remaining vulnerable to palace conspiracy and military abandonment.

Claudius disclosed both sides. The Praetorian Guard found and acclaimed him after Caligula's assassination, making armed proximity to the palace a source of succession. Freedmen and members of the household acquired wide administrative influence. Yet the same reign used central command to extend Roman membership. Claudius urged the admission of leading Gauls to the Senate by recalling that Rome had always renewed itself through outsiders: plebeians followed patricians, Latins followed plebeians, and the peoples of Italy followed Latins. The Aedui entered senatorial right. Rome's ruling body could absorb provincial elites just as the early city had absorbed Sabines and Albans, but admission now came through a prince whose household itself stood beside the old magistracies as an organ of empire.

Nero began by promising to separate palace from commonwealth, restore senatorial competence, and reserve for himself the armies entrusted to the prince. That division briefly operated. It then yielded to the struggles of the imperial family, the dependence of the ruler upon courtiers and guards, treason prosecutions, confiscations, spectacle, and killings authorized or celebrated by Senate and army. The emperor's public performance and private appetite acquired political consequences because no institutional boundary could finally prevent the person who occupied the principate from using its powers as instruments of household fear and desire. Provincial and military revolt ended his rule when the forces that sustained the prince ceased to recognize him.

The year of Galba, Otho, Vitellius, and Vespasian revealed the secret of the imperial order: an emperor could be made outside Rome. Legions and provincial commanders no longer waited for the capital to create sovereign authority; they acclaimed a ruler, marched, negotiated, deserted, and fought, while Senate and people ratified the result produced by armies. Galba's refusal to purchase loyalty confronted soldiers accustomed to imperial gifts. Otho gained the Praetorians; German legions elevated Vitellius; eastern armies elevated Vespasian. The Capitol burned in civil battle and Rome was occupied by rival Roman forces. The city remained the seat, name, treasury, Senate, and sacred center of empire, but it no longer contained the military power that decided who possessed it.

Flavian victory restored stable government after three emperors died by violence. Vespasian joined military success in the provinces to financial and administrative repair, senatorial recognition, a new dynasty, and the incorporation of men outside the oldest nobility. Stability did not abolish the personal and military foundation exposed by the civil war. Titus inherited; Domitian governed through the same permanent center and turned fear of conspiracy into cruelty. Suetonius's sequence of lives and Tacitus's political history illuminate different dimensions of the same order. Biography matters because the constitution attached immense consequences to the ruler and his household; political history matters because Guard, legion, province, treasury, Senate, court, patronage, law, and succession made those personal consequences possible.

This is the fourth older force in the History of Power: the Roman transformation of the city into *res publica*, of public command into territorial *imperium*, and of imperial command into the principate. Rome did not overcome the polis by abandoning civic form. It expanded citizenship, alliance, colony, law, office, road, census, taxation, army, province, and incorporation until one city organized a world no assembly in that city could directly govern. It did not restore kingship by simply reversing the expulsion of Tarquin. It preserved the oath against kings, retained republican names and institutions, and concentrated their decisive powers around the commander called first citizen. Its permanent tensions were public thing and private household, annual office and prolonged command, law and discretionary sovereignty, incorporation and unequal subjection, natural justice and imperial advantage, peace and servitude, Rome as civic center and armies capable of choosing its ruler from the provinces. Egypt had placed the king within sacred creation; Persia had extended the Great King's house across subject peoples; Greece had divided political life among self-governing regimes; Rome took the regime of one city, made its command portable, cumulative, and incorporative, and finally preserved the resulting empire by placing its divided public powers beneath a monarch who was forbidden to bear the name of king.

## The Post-Imperial and Medieval World

*The Two Cities, Successor Kingdoms, Conversion, Chivalric Lordship, and Dynasty*

Roman power did not disappear in a single fall. The western emperor lost the capacity to coordinate armies, taxes, appointments, roads, cities, provincial elites, and frontier peoples from one center, while the eastern emperor continued to govern from Constantinople through Roman law, court, treasury, diplomacy, fortification, and professional war. In the West, imperial titles and institutions survived inside a changing order of Gothic, Frankish, Suevic, Vandal, and other kings, military followings, bishops, monasteries, landed households, cities, and local communities. Political command was redistributed among powers that inherited Rome without any one of them simply remaining Rome.

Christianity changed the standard by which that inheritance could be understood before it supplied a single institutional arrangement for governing it. Augustine wrote after the sack of Rome against the claim that Rome's calamity proved Christianity politically ruinous and against the contrary temptation to make a Christian empire the final form of justice. The two cities arise from two loves: the earthly city from love of self carried to contempt of God, the Heavenly City from love of God carried to contempt of self. They are intermingled during earthly history. They are not identical with state and church, and membership in either cannot be read directly from office, victory, citizenship, or institutional name.

The distinction deprived empire of the power to prove its own righteousness. God grants earthly dominion to good and evil rulers, and military success cannot disclose which city a ruler serves. Remove justice and kingdoms become great bands of robbers, enlarged by victory and protected by the name of rule. Rome's greatness, law, courage, and peace remained real, but its desire for domination could not become the measure of the human good merely because it conquered widely and endured for centuries. No earthly regime could absorb the final end of the human being into its own preservation.

Augustine did not therefore make earthly government unnecessary. Every household and city requires an ordered agreement concerning command and obedience, and even violent men seek a peace suited to their purposes. Earthly peace is a genuine temporal good because embodied and dependent human beings need security, provision, association, judgment, and restraint. The pilgrims of the Heavenly City use, defend, and obey that peace wherever it does not require betrayal of the divine order, while refusing to identify it with the peace in which human desire finally rests. Political authority is necessary within fallen life and permanently incapable of completing it.

This theology broke the older unity between the sacred destiny of a people and the success of its ruling city. Pride is the desire to dominate fellow human beings in place of living under God; no human being is by nature the master of another, although slavery, punishment, and coercive rule persist as consequences and remedies within a disordered world. A king could now be judged by a law and end that neither his ancestry nor his victory created. Bishops, councils, saints, relics, monasteries, and the Roman see could therefore acquire an authority that crossed kingdoms and sometimes confronted their rulers. They did not stand outside property, patronage, law, coercion, or royal protection. The separation of the two cities opened a field in which sacred and political authority could cooperate, penetrate one another, and conflict without becoming identical.

Hydatius records that field at the western edge of imperial dissolution. Alans, Vandals, Sueves, and Goths entered Spain while Roman armies, usurpers, famine, pestilence, taxation, and civil conflict destroyed the security once attached to imperial rule. Provinces were divided by lot, and surviving Hispano-Romans in cities and forts submitted to new rulers. Yet Roman command did not vanish from the record. Emperors, generals, legates, treaties, offices, and embassies continued to act through and beside Gothic and Suevic kings. A ruler could fight in the Roman name while building a kingdom Rome no longer possessed the force to administer directly.

The bishop became a political bearer of continuity because the imperial governor and army could no longer monopolize representation. Hydatius himself crossed the broken field as an envoy to Aetius. Episcopal office joined local population, written memory, diplomacy, sacred interpretation, and access to distant authorities. The chronicle orders invasion, succession, council, heresy, famine, eclipse, portent, murder, and embassy within one providential time. This did not make material causes unreal. It made catastrophe answerable to a history in which political defeat, ecclesiastical fidelity, sin, omen, and divine judgment could belong to one intelligible order after imperial command had ceased to hold the provinces together.

Jordanes preserves the successor kingdom from inside a different memory. Gothic rule began in lineage, migration, kingship, song, martial following, and the remembered house of the Amals. It also arose through relations with Rome. Gothic suffering under Roman officials, the revolt that culminated at Hadrianople, and settlement transformed armed outsiders from pilgrims and strangers into citizens and lords. Theodosius secured alliance through truce, provision, gifts, and honor; Athanaric entered Constantinople, marveled at its walls, ships, armies, and gathered peoples, and called the emperor a god on earth. Gothic kingship did not remain untouched when it entered the imperial field.

Theodoric carried that combination into Italy. Zeno adopted and honored him, conferred Roman office, and authorized the western expedition through which Odoacer was overthrown. Theodoric ruled Goths and Romans, guarded the Senate and Roman people, joined royal houses by marriage, and taught his successors to preserve peace with the eastern emperor next after God. Lineage, warrior loyalty, conquest, Roman title, senatorial continuity, Catholic and Arian division, and imperial recognition composed one kingdom without becoming one principle. Justinian and Belisarius later reconquered the Gothic realm in the name of Roman empire, proving that Constantinople still possessed immense administrative and military reach without restoring the old western unity.

Attila disclosed the contrary possibility within the same world. A king could unite many peoples through victory, fear, tribute, personal reputation, and the sacred sign of the sword, making one ruler's ambition capable of destroying what generations had built. Romans and Visigoths could then fight together against a monarch who threatened both. The coalition did not erase their rivalry, and the defeat of Attila did not restore a single western sovereign. It showed that imperial and successor powers could combine when a more concentrated military kingship endangered every one of them.

Procopius shows the eastern empire continuing as more than a surviving name. Emperor, King of Kings, general, ambassador, fortress, treaty, subsidy, frontier city, tax, court, professional army, and dependent people still formed a transregional political order. The Persian wars required measured supplies, engineering, command, intelligence, negotiation, and the capacity to replace men and money over long campaigns. Constantinople remained a center from which Roman command could reach distant borders, yet its own population, palace, officials, and army made sovereignty precarious within the capital.

The Nika revolt joined Blue and Green factions that had previously treated one another as mortal enemies. Their truce broke prisons, burned the city, challenged officials, and elevated Hypatius as emperor. Justinian dismissed hated ministers but survived only after Theodora rejected flight and palace troops killed the crowd in the Hippodrome. The same reign could administer law across provinces and restore imperial territory while officials sold judgments, changed laws for gain, and stripped subjects through office. The massacre restored the ruler; it did not make the grievances, factional organization, or dependence of the throne upon armed force unreal.

The plague revealed a limit neither emperor nor enemy commanded. Households disappeared, labor and markets stopped, burial custom broke down, and the distinction between ruler and private person narrowed before mass mortality. Soldiers and money from the palace could organize removal of the dead but could neither predict nor master the cause. Procopius allows chance, providence, natural event, human decision, institutional failure, and military skill to remain distinguishable where the chronicles more readily gather calamity into sacred history. Eastern Roman power remained formidable because it could organize bodies and resources; it remained finite because no organization made nature, loyalty, or political consequence fully calculable.

Among the Franks, Gregory of Tours records another composition of Roman, warrior, and Catholic power. Clovis defeated Syagrius, called king of the Romans, but the division of booty at Soissons still allowed an armed follower to deny the king anything beyond his allotted share. Clovis later killed the man at an army review and made royal memory an instrument of fear. The king depended upon warriors whose consent and courage made conquest possible while acquiring the wealth, reputation, and personal command through which their resistance could be punished.

Clovis's baptism transformed the title of conquest without removing conquest. Remigius received him as another Constantine; thousands of his army were baptized with him; Catholic identity distinguished the Frankish king from Arian rivals and joined expansion in Gaul to the protection of orthodox churches and bishops. The conversion was neither a private belief added to an unchanged war band nor proof that violence had become just. Gregory records assassinations, dynastic murders, treachery, and avarice beside miracles, relics, sacraments, and providential victory. The new legitimacy could judge royal violence even while strengthening the king who claimed to defend it.

Frankish kingdoms passed as patrimony among sons and were repeatedly divided by inheritance, marriage, feud, and civil war. Bishops, saints' shrines, churches, and monasteries accumulated land, treasure, sanctuary, archives, literacy, courts of appeal, and diplomatic access. They could protect fugitives, rebuke rulers, negotiate between enemies, preserve civic communities, and condemn taxes that drove subjects from their lands. Kings could also influence episcopal appointments, seize church property, exile bishops, and turn sacred patronage into dynastic policy. Chilperic's complaint that no one reigned but the bishops expressed a real transfer of urban authority without establishing a church sovereign above every king.

Bede carries the redistribution into Britain. Roman troops withdrew while forts, walls, roads, bridges, cities, law, and Christian remains survived without the command that had joined them. British rulers invited Angles, Saxons, and Jutes as military defenders; the auxiliaries became conquerors and founders of kingdoms. Rome's political departure therefore created neither an empty island nor an immediate national order. It left material and ecclesiastical inheritances to peoples whose kingship arose through migration, war, settlement, lineage, and the subjection or displacement of earlier inhabitants.

The mission of Augustine of Canterbury reconnected those kingdoms to Rome through a power different from the departed legions. Ethelbert first granted the missionaries protection, residence, food, liberty to preach, and access to a church used by his Christian queen. After baptism he compelled no one to convert because the service of Christ was to be voluntary, yet he favored believers, endowed the church in his metropolis, and issued written judgments after the Roman model with special protection for ecclesiastical property. Royal conversion did not mechanically convert every subject; it changed what institutions could be built, what law protected, which teachers possessed access, and what path to office and favor opened within the kingdom.

Oswald's invitation to Aidan disclosed another route. Irish monastic Christianity entered Northumbria through a king who granted the episcopal seat, translated preaching to his nobles, supported monasteries, and converted military victory into patronage of a sacred network. Aidan redistributed royal gifts, ransomed captives, trained clergy, and rebuked wealth. At Whitby the inherited practice of Iona confronted the Roman calculation of Easter and the authority of Peter. Bishops, priests, an abbess, kings, and interpreters deliberated; Oswy chose the Roman observance and the dissenting party withdrew. Ecclesiastical unity emerged through royal judgment, but the defeated tradition remained Christian, learned, holy, and politically consequential rather than becoming mere pagan residue.

The Carolingians reconstructed western empire from these dispersed materials. The old Merovingian king remained in title while the hereditary mayor of the palace possessed wealth, office, and effective command; papal judgment then authorized Pippin's elevation, and Frankish election and inheritance placed his sons over the realm. When violence against Leo III drove the pope to Charles for protection, Charles restored the Roman church and received the titles Emperor and Augustus. Einhard records that Charles would not have entered the church had he foreseen the pope's design, but the title nevertheless joined Frankish conquest, Roman place, papal coronation, Christian defense, and the memory of ancient empire in a new western sovereignty.

That reconstruction combined administration and learning with uncompromising conquest. Counts and lieutenants governed provinces and led armies; bishops, abbots, and counts assembled around succession; capitularies supplemented the differing laws of subject peoples; and ancient songs, a vernacular grammar, schools, monasteries, and churches made cultural preservation an act of rule. The same reign fought the Saxons for three decades, deported ten thousand people with their wives and children, destroyed inherited rites, and made baptism and union with the Franks conditions of peace. Bede's Ethelbert refuses to compel belief; Einhard's Charles incorporates a conquered people through compulsory conversion. Both belong to medieval Christian kingship, and neither cancels the other.

The imperial title also created two Romes rather than ending the eastern empire. Constantinople's rulers treated the western coronation as an intrusion upon their dignity before embassies and treaty contained the dispute. Charles protected Christians and holy places through relations with the caliph Harun al-Rashid while claiming the Roman name in the West. The two lives preserved by Einhard and the Monk of St Gall disclose different powers of the same reign: one orders dynasty, office, law, campaign, and imperial title; the other turns Charles into a remembered judge of bishops, abbots, clerks, warriors, the proud, and the poor. Administration and exemplary memory carried the empire together.

The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* continues Bede's kingdoms into a territorial command made through war, fortification, assembly, oath, law, coin, and record. Alfred survived conquest from a small fortress at Athelney, summoned the men of three shires, defeated Guthrum, received hostages and oaths, and became sponsor at his defeated enemy's baptism. Conversion here served a negotiated peace and a new relation between rulers. Edward and Æthelflæd then built and repaired burhs, stationed forces, received submissions, and were chosen as lord and protector by local populations. A fortified network made repeated acts of obedience capable of becoming a kingdom.

Councils, consecrations, and competing memories still governed succession. The Chronicle can record election and acclamation, hereditary title, divine judgment, aristocratic agreement, and conquest without reducing kingship to one of them. In 1066 Harold's succession and consecration stood against William's claim through kinship and invasion, while Edgar's natural right remained another possibility; victory resolved what title alone had not. The Norman settlement then joined castles, land redistribution, sheriffs, geld, oath, ecclesiastical appointment, and an unprecedented survey of every hide, holder, and value. The same chronicler who recognized William's peace and administrative knowledge condemned his avarice, forest law, exactions, and oppression of the poor. Capacity to know and secure a realm did not prove justice in ruling it.

Cosmas of Prague gives Bohemian kingship an origin that is at once election, warning, and dynastic title. Libuše judged the people before their demand for a male ruler; she warned that they were voluntarily exchanging freedom for a lord who would command their bodies, children, labor, animals, land, taxes, and offices. The people nevertheless elected the plowman Přemysl as duke, judge, ruler, protector, and only lord. His peasant shoes remained in the treasury to remind his descendants that all are equal by nature and that royal office should not become arrogant oppression. The origin story therefore legitimates the Přemyslid house while preserving the loss contained in its foundation.

Bohemian church and kingdom arose through jurisdictions that no single authority possessed. The duke assembled clergy, leading men, and people to elect a bishop of Prague; the bishop-elect then sought imperial investiture and metropolitan consecration. Later an emperor, acting in a council of bishops, abbots, and princes, crowned and elevated the duke and confirmed Prague's diocesan claims through privilege. Pope, emperor, archbishop, bishop, duke, cathedral, castle, clergy, magnates, and people all entered the title. Cosmas could therefore condemn the ruler who debased coin as not a duke but a robber and tax collector: sacred office, assembly, and inherited dynasty authorized command while justice in money and judgment limited its rightful use.

Constantinople meanwhile remained a Roman imperial center whose survival required dynastic reorganization. Anna Komnene presents Alexios's reign through the eyes of a daughter born in the purple and explicitly confronts the danger that filial history becomes panegyric. Alexios used marriage, household, ceremonial rank, and newly invented titles such as *sebastokrator* to order the Komnenian family around the throne. Kinship did not replace bureaucracy, army, tax, court, or diplomacy; it supplied the hierarchy through which a depleted empire could regain command.

The emergency exposed a boundary between imperial and ecclesiastical property. An empty treasury and simultaneous wars led the government to invoke canons permitting the use of superfluous church vessels, while clergy disputed the measure and Leo of Chalcedon denounced it. Alexios later presented the taking as a loan made under public necessity, subjected the acts to inquiry, and endowed the injured churches from public funds. The narrative defends the emperor, but it cannot make the defense unnecessary. Church law, synod, episcopal resistance, public finance, military survival, and the emperor's claimed accountability met inside one act.

The Crusades brought Latin armies into that Roman field without placing them outside political form. Alexios demanded oaths and homage, distributed gifts and titles, supplied passage and markets, and claimed the return of cities formerly belonging to the empire. The Treaty of Devol made Bohemond the emperor's liegeman and subject, attached land to a Golden Bull, ordered military service, and regulated succession and ecclesiastical allegiance. Yet the need to bargain with Norman princes and to grant Venetian merchants privileged quarters and freedom from imperial taxation showed the price of survival: the emperor could translate crusaders and commercial cities into Roman categories only by conceding powers that narrowed his own command.

Frederick Barbarossa's western empire arose from a different conjunction. Otto of Freising states that the apex of imperial law was election by the princes rather than automatic lineal descent; Frederick's Hohenstaufen and Welf kinship made him capable of reconciling a divided aristocracy, while homage, coronation at Aachen, papal coronation in Rome, princely counsel, and military expedition successively completed his title. Bishops were elected by clergy and people, invested with temporal regalia by the emperor, and consecrated by the church. Settlement of investiture did not separate two sovereign departments; it left each office constituted through acts claimed by both.

The word *beneficium* then disclosed how incompatible those claims could become. A papal letter described the imperial crown among benefits conferred by the Roman pontiff. Frederick rejected any suggestion that empire was a papal fief and answered that election by the princes gave him kingdom and empire from God alone. Pope and emperor could appeal to the same sacred history, Roman inheritance, law, and image of the two swords while placing the source and rank of their offices in opposite orders. Their universal claims depended upon one another for coronation, peace, and recognition even as each denied that dependence could become subordination.

At Roncaglia Frederick tried to turn the Roman legal inheritance into governing revenue and territorial command. Princes, bishops, consuls, urban judges, and the doctors of law from Bologna assembled before the emperor; regalia were defined as duchies, counties, consulates, mints, markets, tolls, bridges, fisheries, running water, and taxes; magistrates were to be selected by the emperor with the people's consent; feudal service and public peace were written into law. The program made juristic knowledge an instrument for recovering powers cities had long exercised. Milan and the Lombard communes showed its limit. Imperial law could identify a comprehensive right, but enforcing it against organized cities converted a diet of peace into siege, devastation, league, and war.

Matthew Paris records the same contest from within an English monastery and judges both universal and royal demands through their fiscal effects. Papal agents sought a fifth of the revenue of foreign clerics beneficed in England to sustain the struggle against Frederick; bishops demanded deliberation, and royal advisers asked why England was left open to Roman exaction. The king refused to oppose the pope. The account's hostility toward the Roman curia does not erase papal jurisdiction or crusading purpose; it shows how universal government became present to local churches as money transferred out of the realm for a conflict they did not control.

Henry III's request for another subsidy met a comparable record of resistance. Prelates, earls, and barons recited the thirteenth, fifteenth, sixteenth, fortieth, carucage, hidage, scutages, amercements, wardships, and other revenues already granted; they recalled promises that extraordinary levies would not become precedent and that Magna Carta would be observed; and they refused immediate aid while leaving future counsel open if France broke the truce. Parliament was not yet a modern popular sovereign. It was an organized capacity of estates, privileges, counsel, record, and conditional grant to force the king's foreign purposes into negotiation with the realm that would finance them.

Bede, the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, Einhard, the Monk of St Gall, Cosmas, Anna Komnene, Otto of Freising, Matthew Paris, and Saxo show that historical memory had itself become a power of rule. Peoples and rulers entered Christian chronology, Roman documentary authority, dynastic genealogy, episcopal succession, exemplary biography, law, treaty, miracle, and providential judgment. Saxo, writing under the patronage of Archbishop Absalon and the Danish monarchy, carried older northern kings, gods, heroes, vengeance, law, and song into Latin Christian history. Conversion did not require the past to disappear. It required the past to be selected, translated, morally judged, and made ancestral to a present kingdom.

The northern kings remembered by Saxo were neither absolute monarchs nor free individuals joined only by choice. Dan and Angul obtained leadership through the consenting voice of their countrymen and distinction in courage; kings were later chosen by votes proclaimed while electors stood upon fixed stones; royal lineage flowed from Dan; and a tyrant could perish in national mutiny. Kingship joined election, descent, reputation, generosity, sacred or heroic ancestry, military success, and the ability to hold a following. Each element could support the king and each supplied a standard by which another claimant could challenge him.

Frothi's laws made the war order visible. The war-arrow summoned service; booty was distributed by rank and contribution; valor could raise a slave or peasant into a higher standing; assemblies, duels, compensation, tribute, governors, collective punishment, and death established the king's peace after conquest. Law secured possession and restrained private violence while encoding the hierarchy and violence through which the settlement had been won. Saxo's Christian judgment of pagan gods does not dissolve this political memory, and the survival of the memory does not make every ancient speech or deed independent of the Christian royal history that transmits it.

By the world of Froissart, command was distributed through overlapping titles rather than gathered under a territorially exclusive sovereign. A king could be sovereign in one realm and owe homage for lands held from another king. Dynastic inheritance, feudal tenure, oath, marriage, counsel, papal relation, estate, town charter, tax, merchant wealth, and military retinue formed claims that crossed one another. Dukes, counts, knights, bishops, urban communes, Flemish towns, and assemblies of estates possessed organized resources that crowns required and could not simply replace.

Chivalry governed a real but bounded law of arms. A knight could claim a prisoner, swear not to release him, protect him from another captor, demand ransom, honor a defeated king, and acquire wealth through the capture of noble bodies. This code restrained violence among recognized peers and converted war into a field of reputation, contract, and exchange. It did not give the peasant, townsman, woman, or common soldier equal protection, and it did not prevent burning, pillage, siege, massacre, or starvation. When treaties released garrisons, armed Companies retained their organization, occupied fortresses, and lived by ransom and plunder. The instruments of public war could survive its declared cause as autonomous markets in violence.

The aristocratic chronicle celebrates honorable adventure, but the order it records exceeds aristocracy. The captivity of a king opened political space for estates and Parisian government; Flemish cities used money, trade, walls, and armed commons against counts and kings; peasant revolt exposed the human beings upon whose labor chivalric society rested and whom noble vengeance could exclude from its code of honor. Medieval power was therefore neither a pyramid descending without interruption from king to vassal nor an early sovereign state. It was a changing field of crowns, households, fiefs, churches, monasteries, cities, guilds, estates, courts, mercenaries, taxes, customary rights, and jurisdictions whose overlaps produced both restraints and war.

Commines records the effort to master that field through a more continuous princely statecraft. Louis XI preferred intelligence, correspondence, negotiation, division of enemies, pensions, purchased adherence, legal process, controlled assemblies, and patient timing to the single wager of battle. The same methods could prevent a great war, detach a servant from a rival lord, or place confiscated land beneath the appearance of judgment. Charles the Bold possessed richer provinces, martial reputation, and a brilliant household, but rejected counsel, overextended his forces, and destroyed the cooperation upon which Burgundian power rested. Prudence and command were separating from chivalric glory without becoming morally identical to justice.

Commines did not therefore make the concentrated crown absolute. Louis summoned the three Estates at Tours when their deliberation could serve his design and selected men unlikely to oppose him; after Louis's death, Commines defended the assembly of the Estates against courtiers who called it treason and connected extraordinary taxation to the corruption of counsel. He praised the wealth produced by Burgundian peace and light taxation while showing how pensions and offices purchased political attachment. Crown, council, parliament, estates, provincial privilege, town, taxation, money, archive, ambassador, and informer were becoming more tightly coordinated, but subjects and corporate bodies retained the language and organization through which concentration could be judged and resisted.

The fifteenth-century papacy underwent the same concentration under conditions that denied it completion. Conciliar doctrine placed ecclesiastical sovereignty in the whole church represented by a general council rather than in the pope alone; cardinals sought binding electoral capitulations; secular rulers appropriated appointments, revenues, and jurisdictions; and communes and *signori* resisted the territorial construction of the Papal States. The pope still claimed universal spiritual monarchy while acting as an Italian prince whose land, finances, diplomacy, and military security had to be built against other organized powers.

Pius II's *Commentaries* made history part of that construction. Aeneas Silvius had once served the conciliar cause; as pope he narrated a papal victory over councils and princes, portrayed his own humanist education as a qualification to rule, and presented papal leadership as the power capable of redirecting princely self-interest toward peace and crusade after the fall of Constantinople. The account's images of other princes simultaneously defended Pius as temporal ruler. His earlier positions and the institutional weakness recorded inside the narrative prevent the claimed triumph from becoming a neutral description. Papal historiography was itself one weapon in a contest over where sovereignty in church and Italy would reside.

Guicciardini begins where this overlapping order achieved a precarious balance and then destroyed it. Florence, Milan, Naples, Venice, and the papacy restrained one another through leagues, marriage, credit, diplomacy, reputation, and fear that any neighbor's enlargement would alter the whole. Lorenzo de' Medici's influence rested less on extensive territory than on Florentine wealth, public credit, political judgment, and his position among princes. Peace was not produced by one superior jurisdiction. It was the effect of several ambitious powers watching and counterweighting one another.

The equilibrium failed when its guardians died, internal titles and inheritances became weapons, and Lodovico Sforza invited Charles VIII to enforce the Angevin claim to Naples. Papal investiture, French dynastic right, Milanese usurpation, Neapolitan baronial division, mercenary force, and Italian rivalry opened the peninsula to a power none of the inviters could control. Commines, who had opposed the French expedition and then served within it, supplies the counsels, embassies, and calculations of participants; Guicciardini orders their consequences as a connected system. Medieval plurality did not simply vanish into the state. Its crowns, papal fiefs, city republics, dynasties, and privileges were reorganized by permanent diplomacy, territorial finance, and foreign armies into a struggle whose scale exceeded every local design.

Ibn Khaldun supplied this world with an explanation neither derived from the two cities nor confined to Christian kingship. Human beings require cooperation for subsistence and defense, and every association requires a restraining authority capable of preventing its members from attacking one another. Group feeling makes mutual defense, the pressing of claims, obedience, and common action possible. It can arise from blood relation, alliance, clientage, or a relation that comes to operate with the force of kinship. It is not a racial substance and not a sentiment existing outside organized action.

The goal of group feeling is royal authority. A chieftain is obeyed but cannot necessarily compel; a ruler possesses superiority and the power to enforce judgment. The strongest solidarity subordinates or coordinates the others, overthrows an existing dynasty, and creates a new command. Religion can enlarge this power by giving different groups a common orientation, while religious law itself requires an effective solidarity if it is to become a political order. Neither group feeling nor kingship is praised merely because it is strong. Group feeling used for truth and common obligation differs from pride in descent; royal authority directed to public and divine ends differs from the employment of human beings for the ruler's appetite.

A dynasty normally passes through distinguishable conditions rather than remaining the force that founded it. In the first, victory is shared with the group whose solidarity overthrew the old ruler. In the second, the ruler monopolizes sovereignty, excludes his original peers, and recruits clients dependent upon himself. In the third, administration, taxation, salaries, cities, monuments, ceremony, and disciplined armies develop the fruits of rule. In the fourth, successors imitate inherited forms and preserve what they no longer create. In the fifth, treasure is squandered, unqualified favorites receive command, soldiers lose support and access, old clients are alienated, and the foundations of the dynasty are destroyed. The sequence is a recurrent tendency grounded in changing relations, not a fixed calendar that makes every dynasty identical.

Settled civilization is therefore both the achievement and the danger of royal power. Cities, crafts, writing, sciences, schools, markets, architecture, and refined life require population, surplus, specialization, cooperation, and durable rule. Writing carries thought across peoples and ages; great cities and monuments embody the concentrated labor made possible by dynasty. The ruler and city cannot be separated into a political form imposed upon inert economic matter: dynasty and royal authority organize civilization, while subjects, property, labor, tax, craft, and urban cooperation give the dynasty substance.

The fiscal relation exposes that dependence. Money circulates from subjects to ruler through taxation and back through salaries, gifts, purchases, and public expenditure. If the ruler hoards revenue or cuts the allowances that sustain the greatest market, trade contracts, profits fall, cultivation weakens, and tax receipts decline. If rulers confiscate property, impose forced labor, manipulate prices, invent unjust claims, or make acquisition appear to end only in seizure, subjects lose the incentive and means to produce. Injustice ruins civilization and therefore destroys the material basis of the dynasty that commits it. Taxation is not itself the evil; extraction that consumes the cooperation from which revenue arises turns command against its own support.

Luxury names the same reversal within the ruling group. Conquest brings wealth; wealth permits settlement, craft, education, and magnificence; settled abundance weakens the austerity, equality, courage, and solidarity of the founders; the ruler replaces proud kinsmen with dependent outsiders; military and fiscal costs rise as the original capacity for collective defense diminishes. Cities tied entirely to a ruling house can fall when that house crumbles, while knowledge and urban culture can also pass into another people and dynasty. Decline is neither divine abandonment alone nor material exhaustion alone. It is the disconnection of administration, expenditure, loyalty, production, justice, and force from the solidarity that first made them one power.

The Christian and Khaldunian accounts consequently remain in tension. Augustine judges every regime by loves and ends no temporal success can reveal; providential chronicles join victory and disaster to sacred history; Procopius displays the uncertainty of cause and the limits of statecraft; Ibn Khaldun explains recurrent political movement through human social organization while retaining divine law and prophetic politics within that science. None can be translated without remainder into another. Together they show why the post-imperial world cannot be reduced either to faith concealing force or to force wearing religious names.

This is the fifth older force in the History of Power: the post-imperial multiplication, contest, and partial concentration of authority. Roman office, law, city, road, tax, army, title, and historical memory remain active after western imperial command fragments. Constantinople continues Roman sovereignty while a papally crowned western empire reconstructs another Roman claim; warrior kings join lineage, election, conquest, following, conversion, record, and consecration; popes and emperors assert universal powers that require and resist one another; bishops and monasteries turn doctrine, literacy, land, sanctuary, council, relic, and archive into political capacities; burhs, communes, estates, parliaments, and cities organize corporate resources; jurists define regalia that imperial armies cannot effortlessly recover; chivalric law disciplines peers while companies carry organized violence beyond public command; and dynasties convert solidarity into administration, fiscal circulation, diplomacy, territorial statecraft, and decline. Power has no single visible summit, but its bearers increasingly try to make one. Their failed attempts at universality and their more successful coordination of tax, law, information, land, memory, and force create the concentrated crowns, territorial churches, and competing states that emerge from the medieval field without wholly escaping it.

## Early-Modern and Enlightenment Power

*Knowledge, Philosophical History, and Progress*

The next layer begins with a change in the relation between power and knowledge. The medieval world had already made archive, law, counsel, university, chronicle, taxation, and diplomatic intelligence instruments of rule. These powers had ordinarily served inherited jurisdictions, sacred histories, dynastic titles, and practical necessities whose highest ends they did not claim to create. Bacon now proposed the deliberate reformation of learning itself. Hume and Voltaire later turned critical comparison upon the history of government, property, religion, manners, commerce, and the arts. Condorcet transformed the resulting history of human development into a forecast and program of indefinite progress. In this movement, inherited authority became an object of inquiry, the present became an effect whose causes could be reconstructed, and the future became a field upon which organized knowledge claimed the right to act.

Bacon addressed *The Advancement of Learning* to James I because the reformation of knowledge required political magnitude. A solitary thinker could correct an argument; only rulers, states, universities, endowments, correspondence, libraries, workshops, and generations of investigators could reorganize the conditions under which discoveries were made and preserved. Bacon treated the enlargement of learning as one of the highest works available to a king. Royal power possessed the command of persons and means needed to sustain inquiries whose duration, expense, and division of labor exceeded a private life.

The end of this program was not knowledge as courtly ornament, verbal victory, inherited professional authority, private profit, or the satisfaction of curiosity. Bacon attacked learning that pursued words without matter, questions without fruit, and authors as dictators rather than counsellors. He joined contemplation to action and required knowledge to issue in generation, fruit, comfort, the benefit of human life, and works capable of disclosing nature more deeply. The true enlargement of learning therefore changed what counted as intellectual success. A doctrine was not vindicated because it possessed antiquity, scholastic subtlety, or institutional rank; inquiry had to return to the works of nature, correct itself through observation and experiment, and descend again from causes toward new operations.

This active end did not make Baconian knowledge an unlimited human sovereignty. Bacon distinguished the book of God's word from the book of God's works, denied that the senses could discover the nature or will of God, and warned against confusing divinity with natural philosophy. Knowledge became sovereign only when mixed with charity: it was to be directed to use rather than ostentation and to the good of human beings rather than the swelling of the knower. The danger was not only ignorance. Knowledge separated from charity could become a more effective vanity, and action reduced to lucre could corrupt the inquiry that made useful action possible.

Bacon made the institutional form of the reform explicit. The works of learning concerned places, books, and learned persons. Universities and colleges required buildings, revenues, privileges, and government; texts required libraries, sound editions, translations, commentaries, and preservation; existing sciences required teachers, while deficient fields required paid investigators. Gardens, instruments, anatomy, furnaces, engines, mechanical arts, and other experiments required continuing expense. Universities divided by territory needed correspondence with one another, and inquiry needed an organized passage from experiments to causes and from causes to new experiments. Knowledge was no longer imagined principally as the possession of a wise person. It became a coordinated and intergenerational power whose discoveries depended upon institutions capable of financing attention, correcting error, transmitting results, and directing many forms of labor toward a common work.

The relation to political power was therefore reciprocal rather than identical. The state could endow, protect, coordinate, and use inquiry, and the growth of knowledge could enlarge medicine, navigation, agriculture, manufacture, administration, and the material capacities of the state. Yet a decree could not make a false account of nature true. The prince who funded investigation remained dependent upon witnesses, experiments, instruments, skilled practitioners, and institutions whose work had to answer to something beyond the ruler's will. Baconian power over nature began as disciplined submission to inquiry into nature; political command became effective through knowledge only by supporting conditions in which command could be corrected by discovery.

Bacon extended the same architecture to human affairs without reducing them to mechanics. Human philosophy considered the person both separately and in society. Civil knowledge treated conversation, negotiation, and government; rhetoric joined reason to the opinions and manners of an audience; moral philosophy sought inward goodness, while civil knowledge could preserve society through an external goodness sufficient for public action. Education, eloquence, law, counsel, history, and religion shaped conduct before force was required. The reformation of knowledge therefore did not merely add machines to an existing state. It reorganized the formation of rulers, counsellors, professions, citizens, and the public language through which peace and action remained possible.

Hume turned from the reformation of learning to the reconstruction of a national history. Remote origins could not be recovered by repeating pleasing fables. Where written monuments failed, language, manners, customs, neighboring peoples, material conditions, and the surviving structure of institutions had to be compared; where the evidence remained incomplete, conjecture had to remain conjecture. History thereby became an inquiry into relations that no actor had fully designed and no chronicler had adequately recorded.

The first volume of *The History of England* moves from early Britain through the reign of John and interrupts its succession of reigns with separate analyses of Anglo-Saxon and Anglo-Norman government and manners. The form matters. Kings, conversions, wars, conquests, and charters remain, but their political meaning depends upon landholding, military service, jurisdiction, assemblies, church power, commerce, inheritance, personal protection, and degrees of refinement. Constitutional order appears as an historical formation produced by changes in these relations, not as an original English liberty preserved intact beneath every conquest.

Hume described Anglo-Saxon kings as first among citizens whose rule depended upon personal quality, leading men, popular acquiescence, and a national council, but he refused to turn incomplete evidence into a regular plan of election or liberty. The crown was neither simply hereditary nor simply elective; the constitution differed across kingdoms and changed across centuries; and the surviving evidence did not permit an exact delineation of every royal prerogative and popular privilege. Liberty existed as a practice and inheritance without yet constituting the balanced constitutional system that later admirers projected backward.

Feudal power arose in Hume's account from the failure of public government to protect dispersed possessors. Independent landholders surrendered allodial property to a king or powerful lord and received it back under services because coordinated dependence offered more security than isolated freedom. Civil jurisdiction joined military command; offices and fiefs became hereditary; land, service, protection, judgment, revenue, and armed following formed one hierarchy. A kingdom resembled a great barony and a barony a small kingdom. The weakness of the crown did not therefore prove popular liberty. It could leave subjects exposed to petty sovereigns whose private force restrained the king while oppressing those within their territories.

Magna Carta marked a change without creating an entirely new government at one stroke. It established no complete new distribution of offices or powers, but it gave public form to protections that had previously depended upon status, connection, and the capacity to resist. Imprisonment, dispossession, and arbitrary command could now be judged as violations of a charter ordered to general security rather than injuries important only in proportion to the strength of the person harmed. Constitutional liberty emerged through the reinterpretation, publication, and enforcement of inherited claims. It was neither a timeless possession nor the sudden invention of a single document.

Voltaire enlarged the scale and changed the object of this historical inquiry. *An Essay on Universal History, the Manners, and Spirit of Nations* rejects a history composed chiefly of dates, dynastic successions, genealogies, disputed titles, and battles. The proper object is the spirit, manners, and customs of nations, supported by facts worth knowing. Rulers deserve attention when they alter the condition, manners, or happiness of their peoples; laws, religion, commerce, agriculture, towns, domestic life, useful arts, writing, invention, and the ordinary work of obscure persons disclose more of a civilization than the memory of every prince.

This reorientation also broke the identification of universal history with western Christendom. India, China, Persia, the Arabs, and the Ottoman and other eastern powers entered the historical field as civilizations possessing antiquity, government, art, moral teaching, commerce, and intellectual achievement of their own. Europe could no longer serve as the unquestioned beginning or entire measure of the human past. Voltaire's comparison remained evaluative and often severe: he ranked peoples and periods through categories of civilization, barbarism, superstition, refinement, utility, and happiness. The widening of the field did not abolish judgment; it transferred authority from sacred chronology and inherited European precedence to philosophical comparison.

The history of manners uncovered powers hidden by the chronicle of great men. Merchants and artisans preserved necessary arts while princes destroyed one another. Commerce repaired injuries caused by war, towns accumulated capacities neglected by nobles, technical inventions altered daily life, and family custom disclosed the actual level of social existence. A merchant such as Jacques Cœur could provide a crown with resources without which it could not recover territory, and the same crown could confiscate the fortune upon which its strength depended. Political power rested upon forms of production, circulation, skill, and social cooperation that royal command could exploit or destroy but did not create by command alone.

*The Age of Louis XIV* then organized an historical world around a monarch without reducing the age to his private biography. The states of Europe before Louis's rule, the Fronde, Mazarin, the court, ministers, finances, commanders, diplomacy, war, peace, conquest, and the coalition of other powers together constituted the reign. The title of the age expressed the extraordinary concentration of direction, representation, patronage, and military purpose around the crown. The narrative nevertheless preserved the resistance that defined its limits. Civil war formed the monarchy before personal rule; ministers and institutions mediated the king's action; taxation and finance sustained it; other states combined against it; and Louis's ambition repeatedly reopened conflicts that no single victory could settle.

Voltaire thereby made reputation and historical naming parts of power. To give a period the name of one king was to judge that the ruler had impressed a form upon institutions, war, diplomacy, court, arts, and public memory. It was also to submit that ruler to a history capable of distinguishing magnificence from public happiness and military glory from the costs imposed upon a people. The monarch could dominate the image of an age without becoming the sole cause of everything that occurred within it.

Condorcet carried philosophical history beyond comparison into a universal sequence. His ten epochs began with hunting and fishing societies, passed through agriculture, writing, Greece, Rome, decline, revival, printing, the overthrow of authority by philosophy and science, and the movement from Descartes to the French Republic, and ended with the future progress of the human mind. Each present condition resulted from preceding conditions and acted upon those that followed. History was not only a record of what had happened. It was an ordered body of observations from which the laws and future direction of human development might be inferred.

Printing was decisive because it altered the political conditions of truth. Manuscripts could be scarce, restricted, corrupted, or suppressed by the ruling party. The press multiplied copies, enlarged the reading public, preserved discoveries, sustained continued controversy, and made the complete disappearance of established knowledge less likely. Religious and political authorities could still censor, persecute, educate into prejudice, and profit from error, but control of one school, court, pulpit, or manuscript no longer gave the same control over transmission. Reproducibility became a power against the monopoly of authoritative memory.

Condorcet treated the sciences, natural rights, the American and French Revolutions, freedom of thought, public instruction, and the criticism of religious and political prejudice as parts of one emancipation. Perfectibility had no fixed bound beyond the duration of the globe and had passed beyond the final control of any power that wished to stop it. The future could therefore be reduced to three governing hopes: the destruction of inequality among nations, the progress of equality within each nation, and the real improvement of the human being. Education would reduce dependence by making people capable of understanding their interests and rights, judging representatives, resisting imposture, and governing ordinary life through their own reason.

This confidence exceeded Bacon's program, Hume's historical reserve, and Voltaire's comparative judgment. Bacon made progress a labor requiring institutions, experiment, charity, and correction; Hume reconstructed unintended constitutional change from fragmentary evidence and rejected the backward projection of a finished liberty; Voltaire judged civilizations by manners, arts, utility, and happiness while preserving the destructive recurrence of ambition and fanaticism; Condorcet inferred an irreversible direction from the succession and expected social art to accelerate it. Their accounts belong to one layer because knowledge and historical understanding become active powers in all four, but they do not supply one philosophy of history.

Condorcet's universal promise also carried a contradiction within its own language. Equality among nations was imagined as the arrival of every people at the civilization exemplified by the most enlightened French and Anglo-Americans. The claim that no population was condemned by nature to ignorance or servitude attacked hereditary hierarchy and colonial domination, yet the route to emancipation was described through a scale whose present summit had already been assigned to particular modern nations. Universal progress could oppose empire while authorizing those who called themselves advanced to speak for those they called backward. The philosophy of emancipation therefore contained a new possibility of tutelage.

This is the sixth older force in the History of Power: organized knowledge joined to philosophical history and the promise of progress. Its essence is not that science simply became another weapon in the hand of an already complete sovereign. Learning was rebuilt as a network of institutions, paid investigators, books, instruments, experiments, correspondence, and disciplined correction; civil history was rebuilt as the comparison of laws, property, military organization, religion, manners, commerce, arts, and ordinary life; print multiplied the persons and places capable of preserving and disputing truth; and historical succession was converted into a claim about the future improvement of the species. Power now included the capacity to finance inquiry, define valid knowledge, organize its transmission, classify civilizations, name an age, reconstruct the causes of the present, educate judgment, and decide which future counted as progress. The same movement created standards by which inherited rule could be criticized and new authorities capable of governing in the name of knowledge, civilization, equality, and the future.

## Revolutionary and Constitutional Power

*Civic Formation, Constituent Authority, and Regime Survival*

The philosophy of progress reached the French Republic by interpreting revolution as the political overthrow of error and inherited inequality. The revolutions themselves exposed a harder problem. The authority that criticizes an old order is not yet the capacity to found, administer, and preserve a new one. Once the people were named as sovereign, every decisive question returned in another form: who could speak and act in their name, what institutions could convert their authority into government, what inherited relations remained binding, how liberty could survive conflict among unequal interests, and what power would rule while a constitution was being made or destroyed. The American and French experiences belong to one revolutionary field, but they did not constitute one political process or one answer.

Franklin's account begins the American answer beneath the written constitution, in the formation of persons capable of cooperation. Speculative conviction alone did not make conduct reliable. His project of moral perfection translated named virtues into precepts, daily attention, recorded failures, repeated practice, and acquired habit. Temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility joined self-command to usefulness. The project failed as perfection, but its method disclosed a form of power: the individual could become less dependent upon immediate inclination by constructing habits through which judgment endured over time.

This self-formation was inseparable from credit and reputation. Industry and frugality had to be real, but they also had to be visible to neighbors, customers, merchants, and creditors. Plain dress, punctual accounts, avoidance of debt, reliable work, and the appearance appropriate to those practices created the trust through which a tradesman acquired supplies, employment, influence, and independence. Franklin did not place an already complete individual before society. The individual acquired effective freedom through disciplined conduct recognized within a network of judgment, exchange, obligation, and mutual dependence.

The Junto enlarged that power from character into association. Tradesmen and self-taught readers met under rules requiring questions on morals, politics, and natural philosophy, periodic essays, sincere inquiry, and the restraint of contradiction and dogmatic assertion. Their pooled books became a subscription library supported by initial and annual contributions, legal articles, promissory responsibility, and eventually a charter. Reading became a common capacity rather than the private possession of the wealthy; Franklin connected the resulting intelligence of farmers and tradesmen to the colonial defense of their privileges.

Association was also an instrument of public influence. Members formed subordinate clubs, gathered the opinions of inhabitants, recommended one another in business, circulated proposals, and prepared public acceptance before legislation became possible. The same method produced a more equitable plan for the city watch, a fire company organized by articles and common equipment, an academy, a philosophical society, and a voluntary defense association when the elected assembly would not enact a militia law. Printed argument, public meeting, signature, subscription, elected officers, training, lottery, and religious observance coordinated thousands without waiting for one central command. Franklin's civic world did not abolish government; it created institutions and habits through which a free people could supply public capacities, influence law, and make government practicable.

The Federalist began where voluntary association alone could not suffice. The Confederation had failed to supply an effective general government, and the proposed Constitution placed before the people the possibility that political order could be established through reflection and choice rather than accident and force. That choice was never imagined as reason acting without interest. Existing offices, state institutions, local attachments, ambition, passion, and prejudice entered the contest over ratification from the beginning. Deliberate founding meant constructing a government within these forces, not abolishing them before construction could start.

Faction disclosed the central difficulty. Liberty generated different opinions, passions, religions, leaders, and interests; unequal faculties of acquiring property produced creditors and debtors, landed, manufacturing, mercantile, and moneyed interests; and legislation necessarily brought those interests into decisions over one another. Destroying liberty would cure faction by destroying political life, while giving everyone the same opinions and possessions was impossible. Representation and an extended republic therefore sought to control effects that no moral instruction or enlightened statesman could permanently remove. A larger sphere multiplied interests, made a single oppressive majority harder to organize, and divided national from local objects without promising the disappearance of conflict.

The Constitution converted this judgment of human beings into an interior structure of power. Written boundaries alone could not keep legislative, executive, and judicial departments within their places. Each required a will, constitutional means, and motives capable of resisting encroachment by the others; ambition was made to counteract ambition, and the private interest of officeholders became a sentinel over public rights. Dependence upon the people remained the primary control, but elections alone did not oblige government to control itself. Bicameralism, divided functions, different modes and terms of appointment, federal and state jurisdictions, and an independent judiciary made liberty depend upon organized relations among powers rather than upon a command that officeholders remain virtuous.

This was not a design for weak government. The first task was to enable government to control the governed, and only then to oblige it to control itself. An energetic executive required unity, duration, support, and competent powers to defend the community, administer law, protect property, resist faction and anarchy, conduct war and negotiation, and make responsibility visible. Republican safety required dependence upon the people and responsibility for action, not the diffusion of executive authority until no one could act and no one could be held answerable. Limited government was therefore constituted power: strong enough to perform its ends, divided and answerable enough not to become the possession of its agents.

Franklin and the Federalist did not supply identical foundations for republican order. Franklin cultivated habits of virtue, trust, inquiry, and voluntary cooperation from which public institutions could grow. Madison and Hamilton began from the insufficiency of relying upon moral and religious motives when passions and interests organized large numbers. The first disclosed the civic substance without which constitutional machinery becomes empty; the second disclosed the machinery required because civic substance is never uniform, complete, or secure. The American founding joined both to already existing states, laws, churches, property, local governments, associations, and political experience. Reflection and choice acted through an inherited and organized society rather than upon an empty field.

Burke denied that the same language of constituent authority could legitimately reconstruct an entire civil order from abstract first principles. English liberty was claimed as an entailed inheritance received from forefathers and transmitted to posterity. Crown, Lords, Commons, charter, law, property, church, manners, and prescription had acquired their political meaning through time. Their authority did not rest upon the claim that every generation had expressly designed or consented to the whole. Inheritance connected change to continuity and allowed reform without treating the commonwealth as the temporary possession of those alive at one moment.

Society was a contract, but not a partnership in a trade that its present members could dissolve when immediate interest changed. It joined the living, the dead, and those yet to be born in the pursuit of sciences, arts, virtues, and goods that no single generation could obtain. Prejudice, in this account, was not every unexamined error. An inherited disposition could contain the latent reason of accumulated experience, give that reason an affection and motive, and turn duty into habit before a crisis left private reason hesitating. Burke's defense of prejudice, religion, rank, and property therefore opposed the attempt to make naked individual judgment the sole capital of political life.

This did not make every inherited command just or every asserted right false. Real political rights existed as secured advantages within a civil order, but their application required balances and compromises among goods rather than the deduction of unlimited self-government from an abstract equality. The National Assembly claimed a power to regenerate the kingdom while dissolving or subordinating the monarchy, nobility, church, corporations, courts, offices, and property relations that had carried authority, obligation, and social trust. A geometrical and arithmetical constitution could divide territory and assign formal powers without recreating the inherited dispositions and institutions through which obedience and judgment had operated.

The seizure of church property and the issue of paper claims upon its future sale joined constitutional reconstruction to finance. Public credit, land, moneyed interests, confiscation, and the need to fund the new state became parts of the same revolution. Burke's final warning followed from the disorganization of command. Once royal authority, officer-soldier attachment, inherited honor, and legislative confidence had been broken, decrees could not restore obedience by declaration. A popular general capable of conciliating the soldiers could make the army obey him personally and thereby become master of king, assembly, and republic. Constituent power that destroyed every intermediate attachment risked leaving organized force as the only institution able to decide who ruled.

Gouverneur Morris observed this migration of authority from within revolutionary Paris. The Convention possessed the legal name of national sovereignty, but ministers controlled patronage, Jacobin leaders acted through clubs and the Parisian populace, deputies voted under fear, and committees could dispose of the Convention whose authority they formally served. The trial and execution of Louis XVI did more than remove a king. The majority's need to justify dethronement, the fear of acquittal and condemnation, pressure from organized factions, precautions against a popular gathering, and the approach of European war joined legitimacy, survival, force, and foreign consequence in one act.

The Revolutionary Tribunal made the same transformation institutional. It received powers broad enough for its direction to determine the fate of the country, while a proposed constitution could be read, condemned outside the formal sitting, and forgotten. A constitution on paper did not identify the actual sites of decision. Committee, club, tribunal, ministry, section, crowd, prison, foreign embassy, and army acted within and against the legal order, and the people named as sovereign could appear as electorate, Parisian populace, armed section, military levy, silent majority, or justification claimed by a ruling faction.

Finance carried this instability into property and ordinary exchange. Assignats promised value against nationalized land while their multiplication, legal compulsion, restrictions on trade, forced sales, forced loans, and terror altered who could hold goods and on what terms. Government paper bought articles whose owners were compelled to sell and others were forbidden to buy. The depreciation was therefore not only a failed monetary theory. Currency became a relation among confiscated land, public debt, war expenditure, legal tender, commerce, coercion, and the physical survival of owners and creditors.

Morris distinguished this revolution from the American struggle because American institutions, elected magistracies, property, and an existing experience of freedom continued through the war. His comparison came from a Federalist participant, property holder, diplomat, and opponent of the French revolutionary parties, not from a position outside political judgment. His account nevertheless records a decisive sequence: factions consumed one another, committees displaced assemblies, terror reorganized possession, war enlarged military necessity, and France appeared to him as a military government moving toward single despotism. Burke's prediction and Morris's observation converge upon the army, but one states a political consequence inferred in 1790 while the other records and interprets a changing regime from 1792 onward.

Talleyrand's memoir carries the same history through the perspective of an actor who survived and served its successive governments. The Revolution had no single author or directing center. Writers attacking prejudices weakened religious and social principles; ministers enlarged the deficit and popular discontent; philosophical ideas confronted inherited authority; and the Assembly claimed the sovereignty it attributed to the people. The resulting movement exceeded the control of those who helped begin it. Talleyrand's retrospective explanation defended his own conduct, but it also identified why removing a ruler did not reveal one power capable of replacing the whole order.

Within the Constituent Assembly he accepted the revolutionary field while resisting parts of its movement. He supported public instruction, uniform weights and measures, and the application of ecclesiastical wealth to state need; opposed paper money and reduction of the interest on the national debt; participated in the constitutional reorganization of the clergy; resigned his bishopric; and placed himself at the disposition of events. The same action that dissolved inherited institutions created new administrative, educational, fiscal, and religious settlements that required skilled agents to carry them into effect.

His American observations preserved another disagreement within the new order. The federal Constitution restored public trust, secured property and foreign intercourse, and gave the United States the standing of a government among older powers. Hamilton represented an intelligence of national order capable of organizing public credit, customs, commerce, and markets. Talleyrand nevertheless feared that commercial enterprise, banking, territorial expansion, and speculation were outrunning agriculture and social settlement. Hamilton rejected the physiocratic hope that nations could be permanently assigned complementary economic functions. Constitutional order made national development possible without settling whether agrarian moderation or commercial expansion should govern its purpose.

Talleyrand accepted the Directory's foreign ministry because office in a revolutionary crisis could delay destructive orders, soften communications, restore peace abroad, and shape the future from within a government he did not regard as final. This is also a self-defense by a minister who served successive regimes, and it cannot be converted into proof that adaptation was disinterested. It discloses a distinct form of power: administrative continuity, diplomatic access, information, timing, drafting, negotiation, and the capacity to modify a decision between command and execution allowed an officeholder to survive a regime while influencing what followed it.

Brumaire exposed the limit of constituent forms without an effective settlement. Political parties received Bonaparte as indispensable after military reputation and the breakdown of the Directory made him master of the situation. Councils were transferred under a legal pretext, troops surrounded the legislature, persuasion and threats overcame resistance, the Directory was dissolved, and a new constitution replaced one divided executive with another arrangement whose first consul possessed the effective authority of a sovereign. Talleyrand then treated the Consulate as an intermediate form through which France could be reacquainted with monarchical discipline, and he helped place foreign affairs under the personal decision of the First Consul. The sovereignty of the people remained the legitimating dogma while effective power moved through army, coup, constitution, executive hierarchy, administration, and diplomacy toward one man.

This is the seventh older force in the History of Power: revolutionary and constitutional power as the claim that a people can found, authorize, and redesign its political order. Its essence is not revolution alone, written constitution alone, or popular sovereignty spoken as an abstraction. It joins the self-command of citizens, credit and reputation, voluntary association, print, public opinion, property, representation, extended territory, divided office, energetic execution, inherited law, prescription, religion, fiscal capacity, currency, committee, tribunal, army, administration, and diplomacy. Franklin shows how self-government is socially formed; the Federalist shows how conflict is converted into constitutional structure; Burke shows why a living order cannot be rebuilt by geometry after its inherited substance is dissolved; Morris shows formal sovereignty dispersing among organized revolutionary powers; and Talleyrand shows administrative survival and military concentration carrying one regime into another. The people can be named the source of authority, but the history of power turns upon which persons and institutions can organize that authority, preserve cooperation, command resources, survive crisis, and make their interpretation of the founding effective.
