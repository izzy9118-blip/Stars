## Administrative and National Power

*Popular Sovereignty, Durable Administration, Class Conflict, and the National State*

The modern administrative state did not arise merely because rulers accumulated more offices. It arose when the transformation of freedom in modern thought was joined to the machinery created by revolution and war. The first modern construction had begun from separate individuals, fear, self-preservation, natural right, and a sovereign made necessary by their conflict. The second confronted the dependence and inequality produced inside that order and sought a freedom that could be willed, made, and realized by human beings themselves.

Rousseau made legitimate authority depend upon a general will through which citizens obeyed laws they prescribed to themselves. The act of association did not merely protect persons who remained what they had been before society. It had to form a people capable of willing a common good rather than adding private desires together. The sovereign was therefore the collective body of citizens, while government remained its commissioned executor. Popular sovereignty and administrative government were joined but not identical.

Kant relocated this self-legislation within rational autonomy. Freedom was not obedience to appetite or conformity with whatever a people happened to desire. It was action under a universal law that a rational being could prescribe without making another person merely a means. Political right concerned the external conditions under which the freedom of each could coexist with the freedom of all. A lawful constitution could discipline conflicting interests without proving that its citizens or rulers had become morally good.

Hegel denied that freedom could remain an abstract command standing outside the historical and social world. Family, civil society, corporation, public administration, estates, law, and state formed differentiated moments through which private and universal ends could be mediated. Civil servants were supposed to make the universal purpose their occupation, but hierarchy, responsibility, communities, and corporations remained necessary to prevent office from becoming an isolated interest. The state could therefore appear not merely as a restraint imposed upon naturally separate individuals but as an institutional order through which freedom became actual.

These teachings did not converge upon one political form. Rousseau's sovereign people could judge an executive that claimed to speak in its name; Kant's juridical freedom limited what collective purposes could rightfully demand; and Hegel's ethical state claimed a reconciliation that neither popular will nor formal right alone could provide. Their disagreement became historical when revolution named the people sovereign, armies mobilized the nation, and administrative institutions acquired powers capable of acting continuously in the people's name.

Marx rejected the claim that the modern state had completed that reconciliation. Political emancipation made individuals equal as citizens while leaving them unequal within the property and labor relations of civil society. Marx transferred the movement of history from spirit to material production and interpreted law, politics, and administration through the class relations beneath their universal language. The proletariat would have to acquire political power and transform the property relations that reproduced its dependence. Freedom could not be completed by changing the constitutional summit while leaving the social organization of production untouched.

The Revolution and Napoleon supplied the institutional problem in its most concentrated form. Revolutionary sovereignty and mass military obligation preceded Napoleon, but the Consulate and Empire bound inherited and newly organized capacities to a more continuous executive center. The Constitution of Year VIII retained formally differentiated bodies while making the First Consul's decision and appointment powers decisive. In 1804 the language of republic and constitution remained while government was entrusted to a hereditary emperor and officeholders were tied to the imperial settlement by oath.

Territorial administration made that executive relation repeatable. The law of 28 pluviôse Year VIII placed each department under a prefect linked to subprefects and municipal authorities. Paris received a police prefect who corresponded directly with ministers and exercised powers over movement, public order, subordinate agents, places of assembly, and the outward field of religious life. Authority could now travel through appointed offices whose territorial jurisdictions, reporting duties, and administrative records did not depend upon the ruler's physical presence.

Conscription made the citizen a permanent military resource of the nation. The inherited Revolutionary law defined military defense as an obligation of Frenchmen, and the Napoleonic state enlarged the machinery through which that obligation was classified, summoned, supplied, and deployed. Education created another formative relation. State-supported lycées, government authorization, prefectural inspection, municipal schools, families, and special institutions joined schooling to the reproduction of officials, officers, professions, and a common administrative culture.

The Civil Code gave legal uniformity to property, status, inheritance, obligation, and family across the territory while preserving hierarchy within particular relations. Protection of ownership and equality under one civil law did not make every person equal inside the household or in command over productive property. Legal standardization increased the state's capacity to recognize, judge, and enforce relations that remained socially unequal.

Statistics and cadastre made population and property administratively legible. Prefectural inquiries sought comparable knowledge of population, agriculture, industry, commerce, wealth, and resources. Parcel measurement, classification, valuation, ownership matrices, and mutation records connected land to assessment and continuing fiscal records. The territory was no longer known only through local custom, seigneurial memory, or a ruler's occasional survey. It became an object of recurrent reporting capable of being compared from the center.

Indirect-tax administration, public accounting, and roads completed this chain without becoming one undifferentiated bureaucracy. Staffed fiscal offices assessed and collected revenues; the Cour des comptes required public accountants to render accounts across national, departmental, military, maritime, tax, and local funds; road classification distributed construction, payment, maintenance, and enforcement among Treasury, departments, engineers, prefects, municipalities, police, and gendarmerie. Napoleon's correspondence shows the executive intervening in selected questions of surveillance, arrest, information, expenditure, mapping, and public works, but the durability of the system depended upon offices capable of acting when no personal command arrived.

Napoleon's defeat changed the source and form of political authority without abolishing the entire administrative field. The Charter of 1814 restored hereditary monarchy while retaining civil equality, protected property, representative chambers, taxation through law, and settlements inherited from revolution and empire. The Congress of Vienna reconstructed dynastic, territorial, confederal, and international relations rather than restoring every jurisdiction that had existed before 1789. Restoration was a new settlement made from institutions the Revolution had transformed.

Prefectural administration survived the transitions from Empire to Bourbon monarchy, from Bourbon monarchy to the July Monarchy, and from monarchy to the Second Republic. Rulers and ministers replaced officeholders, altered constitutional titles, and redistributed powers while continuing to publish and execute commands through departments, arrondissements, cantons, communes, prefects, subprefects, and administrative councils. The Constitution of 1848 made sovereignty rest upon the universality of French citizens while expressly preserving existing laws, regulations, and authorities until they were lawfully changed. The title of sovereignty could therefore be transformed more rapidly than the machinery through which government reached the territory.

Telegraph and railway systems enlarged this separation of regime from capacity. The telegraph law placed rapid signaling under public authorization and penalized unauthorized networks. The railway law created a national system through a mixed structure of central planning, Treasury finance, departmental and communal contribution, and private concessionary operation. Administrative power did not grow only by absorbing society into ministries. It also grew by assigning public purposes to legally distinct companies, engineers, creditors, local governments, and technical systems whose cooperation extended the reach of the state.

Clausewitz preserved the limit of treating this machinery as an autonomous science. War used organized violence for political purposes, but uncertainty, friction, moral forces, and the changing value of the political object prevented means from mechanically determining ends. Administrative preparation, conscription, roads, communication, finance, and command could multiply military capacity without deciding what political purpose justified its use or how much force that purpose required.

Tocqueville found the corresponding danger inside democracy. Equality could weaken inherited ranks and intermediate powers while turning isolated individuals toward a central authority that promised security, administration, and relief from the burdens of common action. Township government, association, press, jury, and local participation were therefore not ornaments added to democracy; they were powers through which citizens learned to act together and resisted political childhood. Democratic sovereignty could coexist with administrative dependence if citizens possessed the vote but lost the institutions through which they governed parts of their common life.

The revolutions of 1848 brought these contradictions into one field without producing one program. The French Republic established universal male suffrage, separated public powers, elected a president directly, and abolished slavery across the colonies through commands assigned to republican administrators. Emancipation transformed legal status and citizenship while preserving French colonial rule. At the same time, constitutional claims, national movements, social demands, dynastic concessions, armed crowds, citizen militias, armies, and officials struggled across European states over what the people and nation would mean in practice.

The Frankfurt National Assembly attempted to constitute German unity through representative national authority, fundamental rights, a federal order, and a hereditary imperial head. Its constitution did not abolish the member states, and it did not derive national unity from one existing crown alone. Decisive monarchies refused the settlement and military force defeated the campaign to make it effective. The failure of the parliamentary route did not erase the national question; it transferred advantage toward states that already possessed armies, treasuries, diplomats, railways, and administrative organizations.

Marx and Engels identified another conflict beneath the constitutional one. The Communist Manifesto treated the history of existing society as class struggle, interpreted the modern state through the common power of the bourgeois class, and placed proletarian political supremacy within a transformation of capital and property. This teaching did not describe the motives of every participant in 1848 and did not make constitutional rights unreal. It denied that constitutional nationhood and legal equality could by themselves overcome the dependence created by ownership of the means of production.

The defeat of revolution therefore preserved more than reaction. France, Prussia, Piedmont-Sardinia, Austria, and the German states retained or reconstructed constitutions, armies, administrations, and legal relations in different combinations. Some revolutionary claims entered institutions after the movements that raised them had been defeated; others were narrowed, delayed, or redirected. The administrative state could absorb selected demands without accepting the political subject that had first asserted them.

Prussia entered the final struggle over German unification as a constitutional monarchy in which Crown, chambers, ministers, budget, army, and public law were connected but capable of direct conflict. Bismarck's 1862 intervention belonged to a dispute over army reform and parliamentary finance as well as to the German national question. Military power could not be separated from the budget and constitution that supplied it, yet the unresolved budget conflict also showed that executive action might continue without an agreed interpretation of constitutional authority.

The North German Confederation created a federal level whose law prevailed within assigned fields while member states and the Bundesrat remained constitutive. Citizenship, customs, money, banking, commerce, railways, post, telegraph, law, and military organization entered common jurisdiction. The German Empire of 1871 enlarged this machinery rather than beginning again: southern states entered an order marked by federal form, monarchical offices, representative suffrage, and Prussian predominance. National power arose through both central concentration and the legal coordination of states that continued to exist.

Italian unification followed a different route. The representative-monarchical constitution granted in Piedmont-Sardinia in 1848 was carried into the Kingdom of Italy, and the existing Senate, Chamber, Crown, and ministers proclaimed the new royal title in 1861. Political unity expanded an existing dynastic-constitutional state across territories possessing different laws, administrations, economies, local loyalties, and relations to the church. A national crown did not make the peninsula administratively or socially homogeneous by declaration.

The Paris Commune opened a rival possibility at the same historical boundary. The collapse of the Second Empire in war, the arming and political organization of Paris, conflict with the national government at Versailles, and the seizure of municipal authority produced a republic within civil war. The Commune's official declaration did not propose the administrative absorption of France by Paris. It claimed autonomy for every commune, assigned local budget, taxation, services, police, education, and communal property to elected authorities, subjected officials to responsibility and recall, preserved continuing citizen intervention, and sought national unity through the free association of communes.

The Commune did not simply enact Marx's theory. Its members and institutions contained different republican, socialist, Jacobin, Proudhonian, Blanquist, labor, municipal, and patriotic currents. Marx nevertheless interpreted it as the discovery that working-class emancipation could not be accomplished merely by taking possession of the inherited bureaucratic-military machinery. He treated its elected, recallable, working institutions and its replacement of the standing army as elements of another political form. The Commune's declaration and Marx's interpretation converged against inherited centralization without becoming the same document or doctrine.

Versailles defeated the Commune by military force and restored national authority over Paris. In the same year, the German Empire embodied another settlement of nation, federation, army, executive power, and representation. The contrast does not prove that centralized national administration was historically necessary or that communal federation could have supplied every capacity of a modern state. It shows that 1871 contained rival answers to the question of how a people could govern itself at the very moment when administrative, industrial, fiscal, and military power was being organized on a national scale.

This is the tenth older force in the History of Power: administrative and national power as the capacity of a state to survive changes of regime by joining popular authorization to permanent offices, territorial hierarchy, uniform law, conscription, education, police, statistics, cadastral knowledge, taxation, accounting, infrastructure, and organized force. Rousseau, Kant, and Hegel transform the location of freedom without agreeing upon its political form; Marx exposes the class relations that juridical and administrative universality can preserve; Napoleon converts revolutionary sovereignty and mobilization into durable executive machinery; Restoration and republic inherit capacities created under governments they reject; telegraph and railway extend command through mixed public and private institutions; Tocqueville shows how democratic equality can feed administrative tutelage; national unification joins constitutions to armies, federal jurisdictions, dynastic offices, and communications; and the Commune opposes an association of self-governing local powers to the inherited centralized state. The people may remain the declared source of authority while the decisive struggle turns upon which institutions can know, organize, tax, educate, transport, arm, represent, and command them—and whether those institutions make freedom actual or place a more comprehensive machinery above the individuals in whose name it acts.
