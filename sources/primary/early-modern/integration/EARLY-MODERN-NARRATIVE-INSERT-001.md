# SECTION A

## The Early-Modern State, Commercial Republic, and Public Credit

*Founding, Confessional Fracture, Sovereignty, Distributed Power, and the Conversion of Future Revenue into Present Force*

The early-modern transformation of power did not begin when one of the divided authorities of the late-medieval world simply defeated all the rest. It began in a deliberate revolt against the classical and biblical standards by which political life had been judged. Machiavelli lowered the immediate end of politics from the cultivation of human excellence and obedience to an order not made by human beings to the effective founding and preservation of order among human beings as they actually lived; he converted virtue into the capacity to master necessity and fortune and proposed that new modes and orders could replace dependence upon rare wisdom, inherited authority, and chance. Hobbes made this revolution systematic by dissolving the city into naturally separate individuals moved by desire and fear, deriving political obligation from their natural right to self-preservation, and authorizing a sovereign capable of securing peace. Bacon and Descartes extended the same transformation to knowledge, redirecting reason from the contemplation of nature toward its mastery. The institutional reconstruction of sovereignty, jurisdiction, commerce, taxation, and credit was the historical embodiment of this prior change in the purpose of reason and politics.

The late-medieval field had multiplied authorities without producing one uncontested summit. Pope, emperor, king, prince, bishop, estate, city, guild, corporation, household, and military following could possess rights that crossed rather than nested neatly inside one another. Within the new horizon, rulers and political communities repeatedly had to discover which powers must be united, which could remain divided, and which forms of command could survive religious fracture, military competition, expanding commerce, and the increasing cost of organized force. Political order became more deliberately constructible at the same moment that the institutions required to construct it became more numerous.

Machiavelli stands at the opening of this change because he makes the founding and preservation of political order an object of inquiry in its own right. The *Prince* does not begin from the assumption that a catalogue of inherited princely virtues will remain politically effective under every condition. New rulers confront enemies whose position is secured by the old order and uncertain supporters who have not yet experienced the benefits of the new. Founding therefore joins judgment to force, timing, reputation, arms, and the capacity to make new arrangements endure after the first moment of persuasion. The difficulty lies not merely in conceiving a better order but in carrying an order from imagination into institutions among people whose interests, fears, habits, and expectations were formed by what it replaces.

This is the setting of Machiavelli's turn toward the effectual truth of political things. A ruler who judges conduct only by what ought to happen can destroy the state through incapacity to act among people who do not follow the same rule. Necessity therefore enters political judgment without becoming an independent moral good. The prince must know how not to be good when preservation requires it and must also know when such conduct is unnecessary or destructive. The teaching is not that any end licenses any act. It is that political action has consequences which the language of intention cannot abolish, and that preservation, reputation, force, law, fortune, and circumstance must be judged together.

The *Discourses* prevents this inquiry from becoming merely an art of personal rule. Machiavelli studies republics as orders produced and renewed through law, office, arms, conflict, memory, and foundation. Rome's conflict between plebs and senate is not treated simply as a defect which somehow failed to destroy the city. The opposition between social humors produced tribunes, laws, accusations, and institutional outlets that defended liberty. Political conflict could therefore be constitutive rather than merely pathological when it was given forms through which opposed claims generated public orders instead of private war. Liberty and power did not require the disappearance of difference; they could arise through institutions capable of receiving it.

Reform disclosed a second problem. A people formed by inherited names and offices may resist an alteration even when the substance of government has already changed. Machiavelli therefore advised reformers of a free city to retain at least the shadow of older modes, allowing new institutions to carry familiar names and appearances. This was not a theory that all legitimacy was illusion. It recognized that political orders exist in memory and expectation as well as in formal commands. A founder who changes every visible form at once may destroy the habits through which obedience remains intelligible before the new order has acquired its own authority.

The Reformation made this problem of authority inseparable from jurisdiction over belief. Luther denied that the temporal sword could command inward faith. Civil government could act upon bodies, property, taxes, offices, and outward peace; it could not make a soul believe by coercion. Heresy could not be eliminated as though it were an external crime because belief belonged to a field in which the Word rather than the sword had efficacy. This distinction did not privatize religion or remove Christian judgment from politics. It separated kinds of command at precisely the moment when rulers and churches were struggling over which institutions could exercise them.

The Peace of Augsburg showed the resulting fracture in legal form. The Empire did not become a collection of religiously autonomous individuals. Imperial estates and territorial authorities remained the principal constitutional actors. The settlement protected specified confessional estates against coercion by rivals, forbade one estate from forcing another's subjects into its religion, secured a right of departure for persons unable to remain under a territorial settlement, and preserved coexistence in certain imperial cities where both confessions had become established. Religious division was therefore managed through jurisdiction, territory, estate, city, and migration. Peace did not require agreement about truth; it required rules by which rival confessional orders could inhabit the same Empire without making every theological difference a command to civil war.

Calvin preserved the distinction between spiritual and civil government while giving the magistrate an important place in public order. Magistrate, law, and people formed related parts of civil administration; government concerned outward justice, peace, punishment, and the maintenance of an ordered community. Obedience remained the ordinary rule even toward rulers whose conduct was defective. Yet this obedience had two limits. Human command could not rightfully compel disobedience to God, and private patience did not abolish the office of magistrates or estates constitutionally appointed to restrain royal license. Where a political constitution placed guardians between ruler and people, neglect of that office could itself become betrayal of the liberty entrusted to them.

The *Vindiciae contra tyrannos* converted the experience of confessional war into a more explicit problem of resistance. Its sequence of questions asks when subjects must refuse commands contrary to divine law, when rulers may be resisted for destroying religion, when political authorities may be resisted for ruining the commonwealth, and when outside princes may aid an oppressed people. The argument proceeds through covenant, prince, people, magistrate, and office rather than through an unrestricted license of private violence. The disputed identity of its pseudonymous author is itself part of the historical situation: political writing about resistance circulated under conditions in which authorship could carry danger.

Bodin answered the same age of fracture from another direction by defining sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth. Perpetuity distinguishes the sovereign from an official who may possess sweeping authority for a limited time yet remains a depositary of power that ultimately belongs elsewhere. Absoluteness likewise concerns the location of final positive command rather than simple personal lawlessness. The sovereign is not bound by his own ordinary positive laws as a subject is, because the power capable of giving and changing those laws cannot be merely one subordinate office among others. Yet princes remain subject to divine and natural law. Sovereignty identifies where continuing public command resides; it does not make the ruler creator of every standard by which command can be judged.

The Edict of Nantes supplied a different practical settlement. The French monarchy did not solve religious war by making confession politically irrelevant. Catholic institutions retained privileged public standing while Reformed subjects received protections of conscience, specified places and conditions of worship, civil status, and access to public offices. Royal law organized coexistence by fixing which differences could remain without becoming grounds for permanent exclusion or renewed civil war. Religious plurality became an object of administration by the crown, demonstrating both the enlarged reach of monarchy and the continuing impossibility of reducing conviction to royal command.

The Dutch Revolt produced a political order that did not fit neatly into the movement toward one territorial sovereign. The Union of Utrecht bound provinces to act together for defense and certain common purposes as though they formed one province while expressly preserving provincial, municipal, and inherited rights. Peace, war, common contributions, and confederal action required agreed procedures without erasing the political bodies that supplied the union. The Republic's power therefore did not begin in the disappearance of plurality. It began in a constitutional technique for coordinating plurality under conditions of war.

The Act of Abjuration gave that struggle a theory of failed princely rule. Subjects did not exist for the prince; the prince existed to protect subjects according to law, privilege, oath, and obligation. Sustained oppression could dissolve the allegiance which legitimate rule was supposed to secure. This was not yet the naturally independent individual of later social-contract theory standing alone before a sovereign. Provinces and States acted through inherited political bodies and juridical claims. Resistance acquired public force because organized institutions could declare that a ruler had broken the relation from which his title drew authority.

The Dutch East India Company then carried public power into a form that neither medieval corporation nor ordinary merchant partnership fully anticipated. The States General granted a geographically bounded monopoly and authorized the company to negotiate with rulers in the name of the States, build fortifications, maintain armed personnel, appoint governors, and exercise judicial functions overseas. Commercial organization could therefore carry diplomatic and coercive powers at great distance from the European offices that chartered it. The VOC was not a sovereign state independent of the Republic, but neither was it merely private commerce. Public authority and private capital were joined inside a charter capable of turning merchants, ships, forts, soldiers, administrators, contracts, and shareholders into one instrument of overseas action.

Grotius gave the conflict over that action a universal legal form. In the controversy created by Dutch seizure, war, and Asian trade, he asked whether prize could be taken by public authority in a just war and connected the question to commerce upon which Dutch public wealth substantially depended. *Mare Liberum* then stated access to navigation and trade as a right of nations rather than a privilege granted by Iberian monarchy. The universal argument cannot be detached from the national-commercial contest that gave it urgency, but neither can it be reduced to a disguised tariff demand. Dutch interest was defended by articulating a rule claimed to bind Dutch, Portuguese, Spanish, and Asian powers alike.

The domestic Republic remained suspicious of a concentration it willingly exported through its chartered company. Johan de Witt's 1654 *Deductie*, speaking through the States of Holland and West-Friesland, defended the separate constitutional rights of provincial States and warned that hereditary possession of the highest civil and military charges endangered republican liberty. The danger did not depend upon the wickedness of a single officeholder. Repeated inheritance of command could transform office into a dynastic possession and reduce a republic toward monarchy. Caesar served as the classical warning because military force raised for the republic could become the instrument by which the republic was mastered.

Pieter de la Court approached the same order through Holland's material interest. Holland was not simply one undifferentiated land with one interest. Cities, trades, fisheries, manufactures, merchants, workers, taxes, and political jurisdictions created different positions whose prosperity had to be joined rather than assumed. Liberty mattered because people acting under secure conditions could pursue livelihoods whose combined movement sustained the province. Commercial republicanism therefore did not abolish government in favor of private exchange. It asked which public arrangements allowed the heterogeneous productive powers of Holland to reinforce rather than consume one another.

Amsterdam's Wisselbank made monetary reliability a municipal power. Its founding ordinance addressed disorder in coin and exchange by creating a public bank whose obligations were backed by the city. Payment could therefore depend upon an institution whose continuity exceeded the private solvency and personal acquaintance of individual merchants. The later archive of the bank records the ledgers, accounts, and internal procedures through which that promise became routine administration. Monetary order was not produced by a national sovereign decree alone; one city built infrastructure capable of becoming indispensable to commerce extending far beyond its walls.

That infrastructure did not remain isolated from overseas force. Bontemantel recorded the Amsterdam authorities consenting in 1662 to a short-term interest-bearing advance of four hundred thousand guilders from the Wisselbank to the VOC. One transaction cannot prove a permanent policy of company finance, but it establishes the institutional possibility: municipal monetary resources, corporate overseas power, and public decision could meet inside one credit relation. The republic's financial system was not a single treasury. Its strength lay partly in the ability of legally distinct institutions to connect without becoming one office.

The VOC also created claims that could circulate independently of the original subscription. De la Vega's 1688 account of Amsterdam share trading describes purchases, sales, deferred bargains, pledging, bank settlement, rumor, expectation, and speculation around corporate shares. Ownership of distant commercial enterprise became a tradable financial position in Amsterdam. Information about voyages, war, dividends, company prospects, and political events could therefore alter present wealth before a ship returned or a cargo was sold. Commercial empire had acquired a second life in claims upon its future proceeds.

Provincial public credit developed beside this market. Holland's financial archives contain long-running registers of redeemable annuities recording holders, principal, due dates, and annual interest. Public borrowing was thus attached to offices, records, tax streams, and continuing provincial administration rather than existing only as a personal promise from a ruler to a court creditor. In 1671 De Witt subjected life annuities and redeemable annuities to comparative mathematical valuation and tested the calculation against records containing thousands of lives. Fiscal administration had become capable not only of selling future revenue but of studying the value of the claims it created.

Dutch power therefore cannot be located in one institution without losing the mechanism that made it unusual. Provincial States preserved constitutional rights; the States General coordinated common action; cities possessed large fiscal and administrative capacities; merchants organized long-distance trade; the VOC received delegated diplomatic and military authority; the Wisselbank supplied municipal payment infrastructure; provincial treasuries registered public debt; and private traders created secondary markets in corporate claims. None alone was the Republic. Their coordination allowed a small territorial population to command ships, credit, information, armed force, and commercial networks on a scale that a map of formal sovereignty could not predict.

The same distributed strength generated vulnerability. Provincial autonomy could obstruct rapid common action; commercial interest could conflict with military necessity; company power could create overseas commitments larger than municipal prudence; financial claims depended upon confidence that war could destroy; and the concentration of military office around the House of Orange could appear necessary in danger and threatening to republican liberty in peace. The Disaster Year of 1672 exposed these tensions with extraordinary violence. The form that had multiplied capacity also multiplied the points at which political conflict could enter it.

Across the North Sea, England's seventeenth-century crisis began from a different constitutional inheritance. The Petition of Right did not proclaim parliamentary sovereignty. It demanded that taxation, imprisonment, billeting, and martial-law commissions remain within inherited legal limits and parliamentary consent. Crown and Parliament still addressed one another inside a constitution both claimed to preserve. The crisis became revolutionary when disagreement moved from particular abuses toward the control of the institutions through which government itself acted.

The Grand Remonstrance made royal counsel, religion, administration, and political trust objects of parliamentary judgment. The Nineteen Propositions then demanded parliamentary approval over councillors, great officers, militia, and forts. Charles I answered that these claims would not correct particular abuses but transfer the constitutive powers of monarchy to an authority acting without the king. His defense of England as a mixed constitution placed king, Lords, and Commons within one balanced order and warned that reducing the crown to an office dependent upon parliamentary approval would leave only the name of monarchy. Civil war followed because law could no longer decide the dispute over who possessed the force required to interpret and defend the constitution.

Victory over the king did not reveal one uncontested popular sovereign. At Putney the army itself divided over what political consent required. Rainborough argued that the poorest man possessed a life to live and that a person subject to government should have consented to it; Ireton feared that political voice detached from a permanent interest in the kingdom would dissolve property and settlement. The debate occurred among soldiers whose organized force had already become a constitutional actor. 'The people' therefore entered modern politics not as a self-explanatory subject but as a disputed category whose boundaries determined who could authorize the state.

Hobbes answered this world by constructing sovereignty from authorization. Individuals unable to secure peace by separate wills make themselves authors of the acts of one representative capable of using their combined strength for common defense. The commonwealth is an artificial person because the unity of the multitude does not exist politically until representation makes one action attributable to all. Sovereignty in this account may reside in a monarch or an assembly. The decisive requirement is that rival authorities not possess independent titles capable of commanding the same subject into renewed war. Religious, corporate, and political plurality must remain subordinate to one final public representative if civil peace is to endure.

The English Commonwealth nevertheless could not simply implement Hobbes's undivided construction. The 1653 Instrument of Government attempted to distribute legislative authority between the Protector and Parliament, place chief magistracy in the Protector assisted by a council, regulate militia and foreign affairs, and reserve taxation to parliamentary consent. Written constitution appeared because victory and regicide had destroyed the old settlement without supplying an uncontested replacement. Protector, Council, Parliament, Army, courts, local government, and religious constituencies continued to make actual power more divided than the constitutional formula could completely master.

The Restoration restored monarchy without erasing the problem. The Revolution of 1688 then produced a settlement in which the crown remained indispensable while particular exercises of prerogative were declared illegal. Suspension of law, taxation without parliamentary grant, and a peacetime standing army without parliamentary consent were rejected; elections, parliamentary speech, petition, and recurrent meetings received protection. Political limitation therefore did not mean elimination of executive power. It meant that taxation, armed force, law, succession, and government had to move through a constitution in which no single royal will could make every necessary authorization by itself.

Locke gave this settlement a theory different from both Hobbes and the enacted Bill of Rights. Political society exists to preserve property in the broad field of life, liberty, and estate; legislative power is supreme within government but remains a trust directed toward purposes it did not create at will. Taxation requires consent, directly or through representatives, and government can dissolve itself when prince or legislature attacks the trust for which political authority was established. Locke's argument cannot simply be identified with the parliamentary statute of 1689. It supplies a natural-right and trust language through which constitutional power can be judged even when formal institutions remain in place.

The striking result appeared in finance. Limiting unilateral crown taxation did not necessarily reduce the state's capacity to wage war. In 1694 Parliament created duties and revenue streams around voluntary advances for the war against France. A portion of those subscribers, advancing £1.2 million, were incorporated as the Governor and Company of the Bank of England. Public revenue, parliamentary authorization, private subscription, transferable interest, and corporate succession became parts of one fiscal mechanism. Future taxes could support present borrowing because creditors held claims upon an institutional settlement larger than a king's personal promise.

This did not yet create the fully developed modern central bank. The immediate transformation was more basic. War finance became capable of joining a representative legislature that authorized revenue, a crown that conducted war, taxpayers who supplied future streams, subscribers who advanced present funds, and a corporation that organized the creditor body across time. Constitutional limitation and fiscal capacity were therefore not simple opposites. Under some conditions, making taxation dependent upon durable procedures could strengthen the credibility of borrowing and allow a government to mobilize resources that arbitrary seizure would have destroyed.

This is the sixth older force in the History of Power: early-modern power as the institutional realization of a prior revolution in the ends of politics and knowledge. Machiavelli makes acquisition, preservation, new orders, arms, conflict, and reform objects of political knowledge; Reformation fractures make conscience, territorial religion, magistracy, resistance, and sovereignty separable problems; Bodin locates perpetual public command without making rulers creators of divine and natural law; the Dutch Republic converts constitutional plurality into commercial, naval, corporate, monetary, informational, and public-credit capacity; English civil war exposes the inability of inherited forms to decide who commands armed force; Hobbes constructs unity through representation from fear, authorization, and self-preservation; the post-1688 settlement binds crown to Parliament; and funded credit turns future tax authority into present resources for war. Power no longer needs one visible master in order to become more concentrated in effect. It can arise from institutions whose separate limits make their promises credible enough to act together.

# SECTION B

## Enlightenment Constitutional Power

*Moderation, Commerce, General Will, and the Distinction Between Sovereignty and Government*

The enlargement of knowledge and philosophical history did not settle the political problem created by the early-modern state. A government could acquire law, taxation, professional administration, public credit, armies, commerce, and a language of rights while still leaving open the question of how these powers should be arranged and who ultimately authorized them. Montesquieu and Rousseau approached that problem from different directions. One investigated the conditions under which power could moderate power inside an established constitution; the other asked how political association could remain self-government when the people required magistrates to act in their name.

Montesquieu located political liberty neither in doing whatever one wished nor in abolishing government. Liberty required security produced by a constitution in which no citizen had reason to fear the uncontrolled power of another. Because anyone possessing power could abuse it, the remedy was architectural: powers had to be disposed so that power could arrest power. Legislative, executive, and judging functions became especially important because their concentration allowed the same will to make a rule, wield the force necessary to execute it, and judge the person subjected to it.

The famous account of the English constitution was therefore not a command to isolate three departments from all interaction. Montesquieu's order works through capacities to act, prevent, consent, judge, and check. Moderation arises because institutions that possess different social bases and functions cannot easily turn one momentary will into unlimited command. The constitutional lesson differs from Bodin's question of where sovereignty is ultimately located. A state may possess supreme authority and still require arrangements preventing the bearers of particular powers from absorbing the whole field of government.

Commerce added another kind of moderation. Montesquieu argued that exchange could soften destructive prejudices because knowledge of other peoples and mutual need made isolation more difficult. Nations that trade become reciprocally dependent: one has an interest in buying, another in selling. Yet commerce did not simply moralize society. The same exact calculation that restrains brigandage can turn human relations into objects of bargain and weaken forms of generosity that do not calculate immediate interest. Commercial power therefore produces its own manners, disciplines, and limits rather than merely making politics peaceful.

The Dutch experience makes the ambivalence concrete. Trade connected continents and required reliable credit, law, information, translation, and negotiated access, while the same commercial system armed fleets, sustained chartered conquest, fought naval wars, and turned distant coercion into shareholder value. Montesquieu's commercial interdependence belongs inside this contradiction. Exchange can create interests in peace between partners while competition over routes, monopolies, colonies, and strategic access can enlarge the objects over which states fight.

Rousseau began from a different contradiction inside commercial society. The *Discourse on Inequality* reconstructs a movement from relative independence toward comparison, property, public esteem, dependence, and laws capable of fixing social advantages. The account is conjectural rather than documentary. Its political importance lies in the possibility it exposes: institutions that protect property and peace can also stabilize relations of dependence produced before the law announced itself as neutral. A society may therefore become more orderly while its members become less capable of living without the opinions and possessions controlled by others.

The *Social Contract* seeks an association in which combined force does not require submission to another private will. Individuals place themselves and their powers under the general will and thereby form a collective political person. Citizen and subject become two relations of the same member: citizen insofar as one participates in sovereign authority, subject insofar as one obeys the law. Political freedom is not recovered by returning to prepolitical isolation but by constructing an order in which obedience to a genuinely general law can also be self-rule.

Sovereignty in this account is inalienable and indivisible because it is the exercise of the general will. Rousseau therefore rejects the attempt to divide sovereignty itself into legislative, executive, fiscal, judicial, military, and diplomatic pieces. Yet this does not abolish differentiated government. The sovereign legislates only in general; particular execution requires magistrates and administration. Government is an intermediary body charged with executing the laws and maintaining civil and political liberty. It remains a minister of the sovereign rather than the source of sovereign will.

Montesquieu and Rousseau thus draw different boundaries around the same danger. Montesquieu fears a constitution in which the powers needed to make, enforce, and judge law become the possession of one person or body. Rousseau fears a political order in which the executive or representative claims to possess the sovereign will that belongs only to the collective body. The first emphasizes institutional moderation among powers; the second the non-transferability of the general will together with a distinction between sovereign legislation and executive government. Neither can be reduced without remainder to the other.

Their disagreement enters the revolutionary age rather than resolving it. A constituent assembly can claim the people's sovereign authority while simultaneously dividing governmental powers in the name of liberty. A legislature can present itself as representative of the general will while another institution claims that concentration in the legislature destroys constitutional moderation. Commerce can be defended as a school of peace and independence while critics identify property and comparison as sources of dependence. The eighteenth century therefore inherits the early-modern state's capacity and adds competing languages for judging who may exercise it and how political liberty can survive its use.

This is the eighth older force in the History of Power: enlightenment constitutional power as the effort to make powerful government compatible with political liberty by distinguishing the sources, functions, and limits of public authority. Montesquieu makes moderation a property of institutional arrangement and reciprocal checking; commerce becomes a power that binds nations through need while transforming their manners; Rousseau reconstructs inequality as a relation that law can preserve as well as restrain; the social pact makes the people a collective sovereign; and government becomes an executive commission rather than owner of the general will. The problem passed to revolution in a sharpened form: if the people are sovereign, the decisive question is no longer only who rules, but which institutions can act in the people's name without converting that name into a new title to mastery.
