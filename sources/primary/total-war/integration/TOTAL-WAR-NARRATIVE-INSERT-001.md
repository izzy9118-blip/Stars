## Total War and the Mass State

*The Organization of Society for War and Society's Reorganization of the State*

The war that began in 1914 activated the systems assembled during the imperial-industrial age and altered the relation between political authority and social life. Governments did not suddenly create railways, factories, banks, corporations, trade unions, statistical offices, colonial administrations, newspapers, telegraphs, schools or mass armies. They inherited them. What changed was the purpose toward which these institutions were simultaneously directed. Victory now appeared to depend upon the continuous organization of men and women, raw materials and machines, food and shipping, wages and profits, credit and taxation, news and opinion, colonial populations and occupied territories. The battlefield extended backward through every network capable of sustaining it.

Emergency law opened this enlarged field. Britain's Defence of the Realm legislation authorized regulations for public safety and the defense of the realm beyond ordinary peacetime government, and the regime was subsequently enlarged through amendment and regulation. Yet emergency authorization did not amount to a state that could simply issue a command and receive obedience. Each new power had to pass through ministries, local authorities, courts, firms, unions, households and officials whose knowledge and cooperation the government required. The mass state grew by entering existing relations and reorganizing them for war.

German raw-material administration showed the process at its most deliberate. Maritime isolation and dependence upon imported commodities made scarcity a problem of knowledge before it could become a problem of allocation. The War Raw Materials Department gathered inventories, classified commodities, requisitioned supplies, sponsored substitutes and coordinated military demand with private firms and war companies. Resources taken from occupied territory entered the same system. Public direction and private management were not opposites: the state enlarged its power by making industry reportable, comparable and allocable while continuing to rely upon industrial expertise and organization it did not itself possess.

Britain followed a different path. A Ministry of Munitions absorbed functions from older departments, designated controlled establishments and brought contracts, output, labor disputes, worker movement and profits within a special wartime regime. But industrial command remained negotiated as well as compulsory. The Treasury Agreement asked unions to suspend practices that restricted output, accept settlement without stoppage and permit dilution by semiskilled and female labor; in return, it promised consultation, wage protections and restoration of prewar positions and customs after the emergency. The government expressly distinguished control from placing every factory under a general. Existing managers continued to manage, organized workers retained representatives, and the effort to accelerate production created new grounds for bargaining and conflict.

France organized the same necessity through its own military and parliamentary institutions. The Dalbiez law reviewed the distribution of mobilized men among the front, administration and establishments producing for national defense. It attempted to return combat-capable men from protected positions while retaining indispensable industrial skill, and it placed specified retention decisions before commissions in which employers and workers were equally represented. Britain, France and Germany therefore arrived at no single model. Each tried to reconcile the soldier required at the front with the worker required behind it, and each discovered that manpower could not be allocated without political judgments about skill, class, exemption, family, fairness and sacrifice.

Conscription widened that judgment. Britain moved from voluntary enlistment toward statutory military service for defined categories, while Germany's Auxiliary Service Law extended national obligation into civilian war work. The German law also created worker committees, mediation machinery and parliamentary rights of consultation. Compulsion and representation grew together because the state needed labor's continuing performance, not merely its formal submission. Women entered work from which custom or union rule had excluded them, skilled men were moved between uniform and factory, and workers acquired leverage precisely because production had become indispensable to national survival.

The effort to sustain production joined the household to global economic warfare. Britain's blockade used maritime command to restrict commodities moving to and from Germany and drew neutral ships, cargoes, destinations, insurers and trading firms into an administrative net. German food authorities attempted to coordinate increasingly scarce supplies through compulsory administration, rationing and commodity regulation. Britain created a Ministry of Food whose orders reached requisition, maximum prices, ration books, retailers, public meals, local committees, national kitchens, hoarding, waste and overcharging. The civilian meal became the end point of relations running through farms, ships, railways, municipal offices, shops, naval power and military priority.

No administrative chart explains hunger by itself. Blockade acted together with mobilization of agricultural labor, harvest failure, livestock loss, transport congestion, price policy, unequal access and failed substitutions. Nor did rationing mean that governments possessed the food they promised to distribute. Food control exposed both the reach and the limit of the mass state: it could classify needs, impose prices, issue coupons and punish hoarding, yet its legitimacy increasingly depended upon its ability to deliver subsistence through systems already strained by war.

Finance extended wartime command in another direction. War loans converted future taxation into present military resources and drew banks, savings and mass subscription into the state. Excess-profits duties made extraordinary business gains a measured fiscal category without abolishing private ownership. In the United States, the Committee on Public Information made authoritative publicity and coordination of disclosure an executive function. Borrowing, taxation and information did not become one instrument, but together they show the state attempting to command time, wealth and interpretation: future income paid for present destruction, private accounts became objects of public calculation, and public meaning became an organized theater of war.

The war was imperial before it was total. The British War Office counted military effort across the United Kingdom, the Dominions, India and the colonies, arranging persons, theaters, services, transport and casualties within one postwar statistical order. Those categories reveal the scale of imperial mobilization while concealing the unequal relationships through which it occurred. The censored letters of Indian soldiers restore part of what the totals omit. Men at the European front continued to speak to families and communities through languages, duties, fears, material needs and religious understandings not created by metropolitan administration. At the same time, translation, selection and censorship converted their private correspondence into intelligence about morale. Empire supplied soldiers and labor while also watching how they understood the war in which it placed them.

Occupation disclosed a harsher relation. German authorities in Belgium treated unemployment and available civilian labor as problems the occupying power could solve through compulsory work and deportation. Belgium and the United States protested that the measures violated humanity and the law governing occupied populations; Germany defended them as necessary and lawful. The disagreement cannot be dissolved by accepting the occupier's vocabulary of administration or by treating diplomatic protest as the whole experience of the occupied. It establishes that total-war power crossed from mobilizing citizens into claiming the labor of conquered civilians and that the legal meaning of this claim was contested while the coercion occurred.

At the front, the organized means of industrial society returned upon the body. Artillery, machine guns, explosives, trenches, rail supply, signals, medical evacuation and repeated replacement made the soldier a trained agent of violence, a laboring body, a unit in administrative calculation and an object exposed to destruction by persons he often could not see. Official casualty tables made losses comparable across units and territories. Indian letters preserved mediated traces of colonial soldiers interpreting danger and separation. Jünger reconstructed one German officer's experience through exhaustion, fear, discipline, comradeship, material battle, will and honor. His affirmation of the warrior cannot stand for all soldiers, but neither can it be replaced by a later moral consensus. Industrial battle produced incompatible experiences and judgments within the same machinery of destruction.

The scale of mobilization did not make control total. Firms retained management, unions bargained and struck, local authorities interpreted orders, families evaded or adapted regulations, neutral traders contested blockade, soldiers judged officers, occupied people resisted, and parliaments demanded information. Every expansion of command created new organized actors with knowledge or leverage the directing state needed. Total war was therefore not the disappearance of society beneath the state. It was a struggle over the terms on which society could be made to sustain war.

Russia revealed the reverse movement first. In Petrograd, soldiers ordered the election of committees, placed weapons under their control, subjected the Duma military commission's political authority to the Soviet's decisions and asserted civil equality outside service while still demanding discipline on duty. Military organization became a site of dual power. The state that had summoned millions into uniform had created armed populations capable of reorganizing command from within, and the burdens of casualties, supply failure, inflation and distrust widened the break between inherited authority and the persons ordered to preserve it.

Lenin interpreted this crisis as more than administrative failure. The imperialist war had, in his account, accelerated the concentration of capital and state power until advanced societies became coercive wartime orders for workers. The inherited state rested upon separated armed bodies, prisons and official machinery serving class rule; revolution therefore had to break that apparatus rather than merely occupy it and replace it with institutions of armed and recallable popular power. This was not Weber's problem of controlling an unavoidable bureaucracy. It was a claim that the social force mobilized by war could become a new sovereign authority.

The Decree on Peace converted that claim into policy. The Soviet government proposed an immediate armistice, peace without annexations or indemnities, open negotiations and publication of secret treaties. It spoke over the existing governments to war-weary populations and made repudiation of imperial diplomacy part of revolutionary legitimacy. But the other belligerents did not accept a universal peace. At Brest-Litovsk, military weakness forced Russia into a separate treaty requiring demobilization, loss of sovereignty over vast former imperial territories, continued occupation and the reordering of political and economic relations. The distance between the decree and the treaty showed that a revolution could seize a state without escaping the distribution of military power around it.

Germany encountered the same reversal under different conditions. Ludendorff's high command had attempted to join strategy, manpower, industrial output, transport and home-front morale within one war effort. His memoir preserved the commander's view that national endurance failed when civilian politics and the home front ceased to sustain the army. That allocation of blame was itself a political act by a defeated commander. It conflicted with workers, soldiers, parliamentarians and revolutionaries who understood unaccountable command, unequal sacrifice and a war without a credible end as causes of collapse rather than innocent instruments betrayed from behind.

Weber diagnosed the conflict from another position. Modern daily rule necessarily depended upon trained officials, specialized knowledge and continuous administration; no revolutionary phrase or parliamentary vote could make those requirements disappear. Yet expertise and secrecy allowed officials and military authorities to dominate rulers and legislatures that lacked independent knowledge. Parliament was therefore necessary not to administer every office but to expose authority to public contest, cultivate political judgment and select leaders capable of taking responsibility for ends rather than hiding behind technical necessity.

The German revolution transformed these arguments into governing acts. The Council of People's Representatives repealed the state of siege, censorship and most of the Auxiliary Service Law; restored civil liberties and worker protections; promised the eight-hour day; and extended equal, secret and universal suffrage to women and men. Yet the same proclamation promised food, production, property and personal security. The revolutionary government could reject the emergency institutions of the old regime without rejecting the need to administer an industrial society. Power changed hands and principles, but the material relations of subsistence, work and order remained.

The armistice ended combat by dismantling Germany's capacity to continue it. Withdrawal from occupied territories, Allied occupation of the left bank of the Rhine, surrender of weapons, ships, locomotives and vehicles, annulment of Brest-Litovsk, return of Allied prisoners and continuation of the blockade imposed a military termination before a final peace existed. Mobilization did not simply stop and restore the prewar world. The systems assembled for war were unwound through another structure of command in which the victors controlled the timing, territory, material and subsistence of the defeated.

The immediate postwar witnesses disagreed about what this transformation meant. Ludendorff defended command and displaced responsibility toward political disintegration. Lenin treated the war state as concentrated class coercion whose apparatus had to be broken. Weber accepted the permanence of administration but demanded parliamentary control, political leadership and an ethic that assumed responsibility for consequences. Keynes described a different limit: Europe remained an interdependent economic system of food, raw materials, production, transport, credit and confidence, and no victorious government could punish a defeated population without endangering the material order upon which all depended. Jünger preserved the front as bodily ordeal and affirmative warrior experience rather than reducing it to command, class or economic reconstruction. Their disagreement belongs to the history itself.

This is the twelfth older force in the History of Power: total-war and mass-state power as the contested organization of society for sustained industrial war. Its essence is not a state that finally controls everything. It is the conversion of human beings and their institutions into interconnected fields of manpower, production, subsistence, finance, information, empire, occupation and sacrifice, and the counter-movement by which the persons organized within those fields acquire new leverage, loyalties, grievances and forms of authority. The same systems that enabled governments to mobilize entire societies enabled workers to bargain, soldiers to form councils, colonized men to connect front and home, occupied people to contest coercion, and revolutions to overthrow the states directing the war. Power reached more deeply into life than before, but in doing so it armed, organized and politicized the society upon whose obedience it depended.
